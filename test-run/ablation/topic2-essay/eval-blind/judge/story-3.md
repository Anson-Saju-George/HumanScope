The last thing my father and I talked about was a ladder.

Specifically, whether I still had it — the aluminum extension ladder he'd lent me in April to do my gutters — and whether I planned to return it before winter or whether he should just go to Ace and buy a new one and stop asking. He put the question the way he put most questions by then: over the phone, at 8:40 in the morning, before either of us had earned a full sentence.

I told him I had it. It was in the garage behind the kayak, I said, and I'd bring it Sunday. He said Sunday was no good, the Petersons were coming to look at the boat. The Sunday after, then. Fine, he said. Then he told me the fascia on the north side of my house was going soft, he'd seen it in April from the top of that same ladder, and I should get up there and deal with it before the ice did what ice does. I said I would.

I did not. Not the ladder, not the fascia, not the Sunday after.

He died eleven days later, on a Tuesday, in the produce section of the Hannaford on Route 9, which is a detail I have never known what to do with. There is no dignified way to mention where a person was standing. My mother told me he'd had a bag of clementines in his hand. The store manager kept them behind the customer service desk for two days, apparently, because no one knew whose they were, and then someone threw them out. I think about the clementines more than I think about the funeral.

What I want to tell you is that the conversation was completely, aggressively ordinary, and that this is the part nobody warns you about. You expect the last words to arrive dressed for the occasion. You picture something you could carve into granite. What you get is a man asking about a ladder because the ladder was real and the rest of it — the softening between us that neither of us had a vocabulary for — was not something he was going to raise at 8:40 on a Thursday, and neither was I.

We were not estranged. I want to be clear about that, because "we hadn't spoken in years" is a cleaner story and it isn't mine. We spoke all the time. We spoke about ladders, gutters, the Red Sox bullpen, whether I was overpaying for oil, the exact route he would have taken instead of the one I took. He communicated care the way some men do, which is by worrying out loud about your infrastructure. When he said *get up there before the ice*, he was saying something else. I knew that then. I know it better now. But knowing it and answering it are different skills, and I only ever practiced the first one.

Here is the thing I keep returning to. For years afterward I assumed that if I'd known — if some usher had leaned in and whispered *this is the one* — I would have said the true thing. I would have thanked him. I would have told him that I thought about the summer he taught me to drive the tractor more than he could possibly know, that I still tie a clove hitch the way he showed me and think of his hands doing it. I built a whole second version of that phone call in my head, one where I rise to the occasion, and I carried it around like a receipt for a purchase I never made.

But I've stopped believing in that version. I don't think I would have said any of it. I think if he'd told me it was the last call I would have gone quiet and awkward, and he would have hated that, would have found it maudlin and embarrassing, and he would have changed the subject back to the fascia to rescue us both. That was the language we had. It was a real language. It carried real freight. It just didn't have a word for the thing under the ladder, and we had agreed, without ever agreeing, not to invent one.

So I don't punish myself for missing the moment anymore. Missing the moment implies the moment was there to be caught, that it announced itself and I looked away. It didn't announce itself. There was no moment. There was a Thursday, and a garage, and a kayak, and a man who was going to be dead before the leaves turned and who spent his last words with me trying to get his ladder back and his son up on a roof before the frost. If you're looking for the tragedy, it isn't that we didn't say goodbye. It's that we were both, in our clumsy freighted way, saying the only thing we knew how to say, and it happened to be about a ladder.

I brought the ladder to my mother's a month after. She didn't want it and neither did I, but leaving it in my garage had become unbearable in a way I couldn't have defended to anyone. It rode in the bed of the truck with a ratchet strap he would have said was too loose. I leaned it against the wall of his workshop, next to the good level and the jar of drill bits sorted by size, and I stood there for a while in the smell of sawdust and two-stroke oil, and I did not cry, which surprised me, and I have wondered since whether that was loyalty or failure or just the two of us being ourselves one last time in a room.

The fascia, incidentally, is still bad. Worse now. Two winters of ice have done exactly what he said ice does. I get letters from the part of my brain that sounds like him every time it rains sideways. I could fix it in a weekend. I have the ladder.

I think about what it would take to get up there and deal with it, and I think about how the moment I finally do — the moment I climb his ladder and press my thumb into that soft wood and feel it give — will be the closest thing to a last conversation I'm ever going to get. Not the words. The ladder. The doing of the thing he asked me to do, late, alone, with no one to report it to.

I haven't done it yet. I keep the ladder oiled. I tell myself that when I finally climb it I'll have understood something, that the understanding is up there waiting on the north side of the house like a wasp's nest under the eave.

But I suspect that's the same lie as the granite words — the belief that meaning is stored somewhere specific, that you can go and retrieve it if you bring the right equipment. Probably I'll just fix the fascia. Probably it'll take a Saturday. Probably I'll come down, and put the ladder back in the garage behind the kayak, and it will be an ordinary afternoon, which is, I am beginning to think, the only kind of afternoon there has ever been.
