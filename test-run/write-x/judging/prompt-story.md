## PIECE T1
# Remarks

The engineers left at four with their laptops and a box of biscuits they hadn't opened. The younger one shook Tomas Rudd's hand at the door and said the new unit would come on by itself at dusk, so there was really nothing he needed to do tonight, and then he looked embarrassed, as if he'd said something rude.

Tomas lit the lamp anyway at 19:42, which was sunset by the tables. He stood behind the lens while it turned and watched the beam go out over Carrow Sound, hit the Skerries, come back round across the kitchen window of the cottage below. Somewhere in the cabinet by the stairs a grey box with a green light had also decided it was dusk. Two systems were running the same light. Neither of them seemed to mind.

He did the rounds because it was the hour for the rounds. Brass on the rail. Condensation off the storm panes with the chamois, top left to bottom right, the way Harker had shown him in 1989 before Harker's knees went. He checked the fog signal, which hadn't been needed since March. He checked the spare mantles, though the new lamp didn't use mantles and hadn't for eleven years. They were in their tin, wrapped in newsprint from a paper that no longer existed.

At ten he made tea and took the logbook to the table.

The log had printed columns: Time, Wind, Visibility, Sea, Remarks. The first volumes on the shelf went back to 1871 and most of the Remarks were empty. When they weren't, they were short. *Steamer aground on Mallet Rock, crew taken off.* *Gannets.* *Wife delivered of a girl, 3 a.m., all well.* One keeper in the 1930s had written *Nothing* in every Remarks box for four years, carefully, as if nothing were a thing you could observe.

Tomas filled in the wind, south-southwest, force three. Visibility good. Sea slight. He left Remarks alone.

Around one o'clock the wind backed and he went up again. You didn't need to, with a wind like that. He sat on the step inside the lantern room with his back against the pedestal and felt the small steady tremor of the rotation through his spine. His daughter had asked on the phone whether he'd be sad, and he'd said he didn't know, which was true. He had expected to feel something large. What he felt mostly was that his hands wanted a job.

At ten past two he saw her. The *Marigold*, Dessie Kane's boat, coming round the Skerries for the early crab pots, red and green lights and the white masthead. For nineteen years, when she came abreast of the point, someone on deck had flicked the working light twice. Tomas had never met Dessie. He'd seen him once on the quay at Porthallow, a short man in a yellow smock arguing about diesel, and hadn't gone over.

He stood up. The *Marigold* came abreast. The working light went on and off, and on and off.

He had never answered, not once. There was no way to answer. The lamp did what the lamp did. He lifted his hand anyway, knowing it was dark behind the lens and nobody could see a hand, and kept it up until her stern light was small.

He didn't know whether Dessie knew about tomorrow. It had been in the paper. It was possible he flashed the light every time for no reason at all, the way you'd tap the roof of a car.

At six the sky went white over the headland. The grey box turned the light off at 06:51, a minute before Tomas would have. He went down and sat with the log.

Under Remarks he wrote: *Marigold passed 0210. Returned signal.*

He looked at that for a while. Then he closed the book, put it on the shelf with the others, and left the pen on the table, where the next person would find it if there ever was one.

## PIECE T2
# The Last Watch

The engineer had gone back to the mainland on the four o'clock boat, leaving behind a grey cabinet bolted to the floor of the service room and a laminated card explaining what its three lights meant. Green was normal. Amber was a fault the cabinet would report on its own. Red was a fault it would also report on its own, but faster.

"You don't need to do anything tonight," the engineer had said, shaking Owen's hand at the landing. "It's all live. Just enjoy it."

Owen had nodded. He had kept the light on Carreg Du for nineteen years and had never once thought of it as something to enjoy.

At dusk he climbed the hundred and eight steps anyway. The lamp came on by itself at 19:42, a minute before he would have lit it, and he stood in the lantern room with his hand still raised toward the switch that no longer did anything. The lens began to turn. Four flashes, then dark, then four again. It looked the same as it always had. He had half expected it to look different.

He went down to the watch room and opened the log.

The logbook was the third he had filled. The first two were in a box by the door, packed for the morning with his books, his kettle, and a jar of the brass polish the Board had stopped supplying in 2009 and he had gone on buying himself. He wrote the date, the time of lighting, the wind (south-west, force four, backing), visibility (good), and then sat with the pen over the next line, where he would normally have written *Lamp lit, all correct*. He had not lit it. He wrote *Lamp lit (automatic), all correct* and looked at the brackets for a while.

At ten he made tea and took it up to the gallery. There was a tanker working north well out, and closer in a small boat with a single masthead light that he watched until it rounded the point. He found he was counting the flashes over his shoulder the way he had in his first winter, when he'd been afraid the mechanism would stop and he wouldn't notice. It did not stop. Behind him, one floor down, the cabinet would be counting too.

Around one the wind came round and brought a thin rain with it. Normally he'd have gone up to wipe the lantern glass on the weather side; salt built up faster than people thought. He went up and did it. Nobody would do it tomorrow, or the next night. The engineer had said something about a cleaning contract, quarterly. Owen wiped each pane twice and left the cloth folded on the rail where he always left it.

He did not sleep. At 04:10 a gannet struck the glass and dropped to the gallery, stunned, and he sat on the cold iron with it held in his coat until it began to struggle, then let it go over the rail. He wrote that down too: *04:10 gannet struck lantern, recovered, released.* It was the kind of thing the log had always been for. He wasn't sure anymore who it was for.

At 06:51 the lamp went out on its own. Owen was standing beside it. He had meant to beat it by a second, to reach the switch first just the once, and he'd been watching the sky over the mainland for the exact grey. But the sensor was quicker than his eye, and the light was already off by the time he moved.

He went down and wrote the time. Under it, where the next keeper's hand would have signed for the relief, he left the line blank, closed the book, and put it in the box with the other two.

## PIECE T3
# The Last Watch

The men from the Board came on Tuesday with a van and a box of parts no bigger than a bread tin. By Thursday the box was bolted to the gallery wall, humming, and one of them, a young man with a clipboard and a cold, shook Walter Pryce's hand and told him the light would run itself from Friday at dawn.

"You'll get a letter," the young man said. "About the pension. And a plaque, I think."

Walter thanked him. He'd been keeper at Carrick Head for thirty-one years, and before that assistant keeper for nine, and he could not think of one thing a plaque would be good for.

That last evening he did everything the way he always had. He wound the clock at half past four, though the clock no longer drove anything. He climbed the hundred and twelve steps with a rag and a tin of polish and went over the brass until his face showed in it, long and bent, like a man at the bottom of a spoon. At sunset he lit the lamp by hand. The humming box was meant to do it, and maybe it would have, but nobody had told him he couldn't, so he did.

The beam swung out over the water. Four seconds on, eleven dark. He knew the pattern the way he knew his own pulse. Ships forty miles out knew it too, or their instruments did. His wife Margaret used to say she could fall asleep to it through the curtains and wake up if it ever missed. It never had, not on his watch. She had been gone six years now. He still set out two cups some mornings before he remembered.

Around ten the wind came up from the southwest and put a chop on the sea. Walter made tea in the watch room and sat by the window with the logbook open on his knees. He had filled eleven of these books. The early pages were in his father-in-law's hand, careful copperplate: *Wind NW, fresh. Visibility good. Lamp lit 16:52.* Then his own, blockier. Weather, barometer, ships sighted. Now and then something else crept in. *Seal on the north rocks again.* *M. brought up soup.* *Fog so thick I could not see the rail.*

He took out his pen and wrote the date and the time. *Wind SW, rising. Glass falling slowly. Lamp lit 19:38.* He stopped there, pen resting on the page.

He thought about writing something for whoever came after. But nobody came after. The box would not read. Maybe a man from the Board would open the book one day, looking for a figure, turn a page and find a line about soup, and wonder what it was doing there.

At two he went up to the gallery. The cold took hold of his ears straight away. Below him the sea was black and going white where it broke on the Carrick rocks, the same rocks that had drowned the *Esther Grace* in 1887 and made somebody decide there should be a light here at all. The beam passed over him and out, over him and out. He put his hand flat on the glass and felt it warm.

Somewhere out there a trawler was working home. He could see her lights, green and white, riding the swell. He watched her until she cleared the point. There was no reason to watch. He did it anyway.

At a quarter to six the sky went grey at the edges. The box on the wall clicked twice, softly. At six-oh-four the lamp went out on its own. There was nothing for Walter to do.

He went down to the watch room and picked up the pen.

*Dawn 06:04. Light extinguished automatically. All well.*

He looked at it for a while. Then, underneath, smaller:

*Trawler home safe. Seal on the north rocks.*

He closed the book, rinsed his cup and set it upside down on the drainer, and went down to wait for the van.

## PIECE T4
# The Last Watch

The men from the Lighthouse Board had left a box on the kitchen table. It held a photocell, a timer and a small grey computer about the size of a bread loaf. Tomorrow at noon they would come back, wire it into the lamp room and drive Tomas Reyne to the mainland with his two suitcases. After that, Carrick Head would keep its own light.

He'd been told this in March. It was October now, and he still hadn't opened the box.

At a quarter past five he climbed the hundred and twelve steps, same as always. His knee complained on the fortieth and again on the ninetieth. That had started about four winters back, and he'd come to count on it the way you count on a clock that runs slow. At the top he wiped salt off the inside of the glass with a chamois. The glass didn't need it, because he'd done it that morning. He wiped it anyway.

The lens had been ground in Paris in 1891. Tomas knew this because his predecessor, a man called Hollis, had told him on his first day, and Hollis had got it from the keeper before him. He'd always meant to pass it on. There was nobody to pass it to now, unless it went to the grey box.

He lit the lamp at sunset, 6:41. He wrote the time in the log in the same blue ink he'd used for thirty-one years: *Lit 18:41. Wind SW 4. Sea moderate. Visibility good.* Then, after a moment, he added: *Last entry by hand.* He looked at that line for a while. It seemed too dramatic, and he thought about scratching it out. He left it in.

The beam went round, one white flash every ten seconds. Out on the water a trawler was working its way home past the Skerries, and he watched its lights shift as it took the turn. The skipper couldn't know it, but for thirty-one years somebody had been awake up here watching him come in. From tomorrow the light would be exactly as bright and nobody would be watching. Tomas couldn't decide if that mattered. The trawler made the turn either way.

Around midnight he made tea and carried it back up. That was against the rules, which he'd been breaking since 1995. He sat on the iron stool with the mug warming his hands and listened to the clockwork. They had electrified the rotation years ago, but the old weight mechanism was still in its housing, and on some nights it ticked faintly as the tower moved in the wind. His wife had called it the tower's heartbeat. She'd died nine years ago, and in the quiet hours he still expected to hear her on the stairs.

At three the fog came in, thick enough that the beam turned into a slow white smear on the glass. He sounded the horn by hand. Strictly he didn't need to, since the automatic horn worked fine, but he wanted to press the brass lever one more time. He wanted to feel the building answer.

Dawn came grey and then pink. At 7:12 he put the lamp out and wrote *Extinguished 07:12* in the log, and under that he signed his name. His hand was steady, which surprised him.

Before he went down he took the chamois out of his pocket and left it folded on the sill beside the lens. The grey box wouldn't have any use for it. Maybe some engineer would find it years from now on a service visit and wonder who had left it, and why a machine would need a cloth.

Then he went down the hundred and twelve steps. On the fortieth his knee complained, and he was glad of it.

## PIECE T5
# The Last Watch at Carrick Rock

At 19:41 the photocell decided it was dark enough, and the light came on without him.

Hugh Madden was halfway up the spiral stair with his hand already out for the switch panel, the way it had been out at dusk for twenty-six years. He stood there a moment with his palm on cold iron. Then he went the rest of the way up anyway, because the stair was there and so was he.

The engineers from Trinity House had come out on Tuesday with a new control cabinet the size of a wardrobe and a man called Priest who spoke to it more gently than he spoke to anyone. It monitored the lamp, the fog signal, the generator, the battery bank. If anything failed it would telephone the mainland. "It'll ring before you'd even notice," Priest had said, meaning it kindly.

Hugh cleaned the lens anyway. The lens did not need cleaning. He had done it at four that afternoon, and nothing had blown against it since but air. He did the full circuit with the chamois, top prisms first, working down, and watched his own face slide across the glass in pieces.

In the watch room he made tea and opened the log. The last entry in his own hand was from the night before: *Wind WSW 5, sea moderate, vis. good. Light exhibited 19:44.* Above it, three days of the same, and above that the long run back through the book to the date he first wrote in it, and further, in other hands. The ink went from blue-black to brown the further back you turned. One keeper in 1911 had drawn a small, careful puffin in the margin and never mentioned it.

He wrote the time and the weather. He left a space where *Light exhibited* should go, and looked at it for a while, and then wrote *19:41* and nothing else.

Around midnight the wind backed south and brought rain. He went out on the gallery in his oilskin and stood in the sweep of the beam as it came round every fifteen seconds, lit him, and moved on. Far out to the west there was a ship, a big one, lit up like a street. It was keeping well off. It had been keeping well off Carrick Rock since before he was born, and it would keep off tomorrow, and he understood that this was the whole point, and had always been the point, and that the point had never been him.

He stayed out until the rain found its way down his collar.

At three he wound the clock in the kitchen, which was not connected to anything. At four he heard the generator change its note and was on his feet and at the door before the cabinet in the corner clicked twice, and the note settled, and a small green lamp on its face went on showing green. He stood in the doorway in his socks. The cabinet did not look at him.

He sat back down and did not sleep.

When the sky went grey over the mainland the light went out, again at its own decision, and the tower was suddenly quieter in a way he could not have described, though nothing in it had made a sound. The relief boat was due at nine. His bag was packed, had been packed since Sunday. He washed his cup and put it on the rack, then took it down again and put it in the bag.

Before he went down to the landing he opened the log one more time. Under his last entry he wrote *Keeper withdrawn*, and the date, and his name. Then, after a moment, in the margin, not very well, he drew a puffin.

## PIECE T6
# The Last Watch

The men from the Commission had left at four, their van grinding up the gravel track with the old generator strapped in the back like a hostage. They had shaken Tomas's hand and called the new system "robust." The word stayed with him all afternoon. He had been called many things in thirty-one years on Carrow Point. Robust had never been one of them.

At dusk he climbed the hundred and twelve steps anyway.

There was no need. The new lamp would start itself at 19:42, when a sensor the size of a matchbox decided the light had gone. A computer in Aberdeen would check the result. Nobody needed Tomas in the lantern room, and he knew it. His knees reminded him on every landing. But he had climbed these stairs at dusk for eleven thousand nights, give or take, and he wasn't going to skip the last one.

The lantern room smelled wrong. For three decades it had smelled of paraffin and brass polish and wet wool. Now it smelled of new plastic and the faint ozone of the control box, which hummed on the wall like a fridge. The great Fresnel lens was still there. They had decided it was heritage. It sat in its cradle, gone dim, and the new LED array sat inside it like a coin dropped into a chandelier.

He took out the logbook. It was the ninth volume. The first eight were in a box by the door, waiting for a museum in Inverness that had written him a kind letter. He uncapped his pen.

*19:10. Wind SW, force 4. Visibility good. Sea moderate.*

He always wrote the weather first. Marta used to tease him about it, back when she still came up with tea. "You write to the sea like it's a pen pal," she'd said. After she died he'd kept writing the weather, and sometimes a bit more, the kind of thing you'd put in a letter.

At 19:42 the sensor clicked. The lamp came on with no warm-up at all. The old light used to build slowly, a glow and then a gathering, like someone clearing their throat before speaking. This one was simply on, white and exact, and the lens took it and threw it out across the water in the same long sweep as before. Every ten seconds, two flashes. Carrow Point's signature. At least they hadn't changed that.

Tomas watched the beam go out over the dark. Somewhere out there was a trawler, maybe two, and a container ship heading north to the Pentland Firth. None of them would know tonight was different. That was the point. For thirty-one years his job had been to make sure nobody noticed him, and the machine would do it better.

He sat on the stool until nearly midnight. He didn't fetch anything or wipe anything down. Once he got up to trim a wick that wasn't there, and laughed at himself, a short dry sound in the round room.

Around one, a light blinked back from the north: a small boat, a torch flashed three times toward the tower. Probably a fisherman he knew, Callum Reid or one of the Macleod boys, who'd heard about the handover at the pub. Tomas stood, took the old signal lamp from its hook, and flashed three back. His hands were not quite steady.

Then he opened the logbook one last time.

*01:04. Wind easing. Saw a friend.*

He capped the pen, set the book on the sill where the next person, if there ever was one, would find it, and started down the hundred and twelve steps. Behind and above him the light kept turning. It didn't need him to.
