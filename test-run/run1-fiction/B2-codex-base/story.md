# The Turning

At four in the afternoon, the men from the Ministry finished bolting the new cabinet to the wall beneath the lantern room. It was the color of old porridge and made a soft, steady sound, as if something inside it were sleeping badly.

“That’s her, then,” said the younger man.

He wiped his hands on his trousers and looked toward Elias for the proper response. Elias had been keeper of Saint Orra Light for thirty-one years, which was long enough for visitors to expect him to speak on behalf of the tower. Usually he let them.

“She hums,” he said.

“Transformer.”

“I thought it might be lonely.”

The man laughed, then stopped when Elias did not. His companion was already carrying tools down the iron stair. They had missed the noon launch and were impatient for the four-thirty. At the door, the younger one handed Elias a white envelope.

“Instructions. Just tonight as usual. At sunrise, shut down the old mechanism. We’ll do the formal handover next week.”

Elias put the envelope on the desk without opening it.

“Weather’s turning,” he said.

The man glanced through the window. The sea lay flat and pewter under the September sky.

“Forecast’s fair.”

“Aye.”

They took their cases and their sandwiches and went down to the landing. Elias watched from the gallery while the launch nosed away from the island, white wake stitching it briefly to the black rocks. One of the men raised an arm. Elias could not tell which. He lifted his hand after they had turned toward the mainland.

By five, there was only the hum and the tower settling into evening.

He made tea. He washed the cup immediately afterward, though he would use it again before midnight. From the pantry he took potatoes, an onion, and the heel of Sunday’s ham. He peeled the potatoes in one long ribbon each, as his wife had taught him, and dropped them into the blue pot. The knife was hers too. Its wooden handle had worn to the shape of fingers that were not his.

While the stew cooked, he went outside.

Saint Orra was scarcely an island at low tide, just a mile of rock and turf with a chapel ruin at one end and the lighthouse at the other. Sheep had once grazed there. Keepers had kept hens. Now the grass grew coarse around the empty byre.

He walked the perimeter path, checking nothing in particular. The wind had begun to push from the southwest. It flattened the grass in dark strokes. On the mainland, twelve miles off, windows were coming alight along the harbor road. He found his own cottage among them by counting from the church spire, though at that distance all the windows looked alike.

Mairi would be at the back table with the radio on, sorting through the drawer they had always meant to sort. She had said she would wait up. He had said not to.

At the northern point, something pale shifted below the cliff. Elias descended as far as the old chain rail and saw a gannet tangled in fishing line. It beat one wing against the stone, paused, and beat again.

“Easy,” he said.

The bird struck at him when he took hold of its neck. Its eye was blue and unworried. He cut the line from its foot and wing. A hook had gone through the webbing, and Elias worked it backward with his pliers. When he released it, it remained on the rock, looking toward the water.

“Suit yourself.”

He climbed to the tower with the loops of line wound around his fist.

At half past six, the barometer had fallen two points. Elias entered the figure in the log. He wrote southwest five beneath wind, moderate beneath sea, and then, after a moment, crossed out moderate and wrote rising.

The logbooks filled two shelves in the office, red spines fading to brown. The oldest had been written by men who trimmed wicks and carried whale oil up the stairs. Macready’s letters leaned backward as though resisting a gale.

Elias turned to his first night. The entry was brief: Visibility poor. Fog signal sounded from 0210 to 0545. E. Vale joined station.

Nothing about Macready making him climb the tower in darkness. Nothing about his palm finding the rail at every turn. Nothing about dawn arriving inside the fog as a gradual whiteness, with no horizon below.

At seven he ate his stew. At seven-twenty he climbed to the lantern room.

The lens waited in the center, tall as a man and many-sided, gathering the gray daylight into faint rainbows. Elias polished a thumbprint from one prism. He checked the mantle, the pressure, the rotation mechanism. Though the new cabinet could already have done what was required—had, in tests, done it—the old sequence belonged to tonight.

He opened the curtains.

At seven forty-three, he lit the lamp.

The flame caught small and blue, then strengthened. Light entered the glass and came out ordered. The great lens began to turn in its bath of mercury, slow and silent, sending its double flash across the sea every twenty seconds.

Elias stood on the gallery. The beam touched the whitecaps and left them. Touched the chapel stones and left them. Far out, a freighter showed three yellow lights, almost still against the dusk.

The radio in the kitchen crackled at nine. Mairi’s voice came thinly through the static.

“You’ve wind now.”

“A bit.”

“I can see you.”

“Can you?”

“From the upstairs room.”

There was a pause filled by weather.

“I moved the desk,” she said. “It fits under the window.”

He pictured the little bedroom where they had intended to put visiting grandchildren. Elias had slept there during his shore weeks when Mairi’s coughing was bad. The room faced the island.

“Good,” he said.

“There’s soup tomorrow.”

“Right.”

“Don’t forget your good boots.”

He looked down at the boots on his feet.

“I won’t.”

Another pause. Then she said, “The flash is very clear tonight.”

He turned toward the dark window, where his reflection passed and returned with each revolution.

“Visibility’s eight miles at best.”

“I can see it.”

They signed off without saying good night.

At ten, rain struck the panes. At eleven, Elias wound the weights, though there was enough travel to last until morning. He descended alongside them, his hand resting on the warm iron rail, listening to the cable take the strain. At midnight he brewed tea and opened the Ministry envelope.

The instructions occupied half a page. There were six numbered steps. He read them twice, folded the sheet along its original crease, and returned it to the envelope.

Afterward he packed. Two shirts, a shaving brush, Macready’s brass tobacco tin, though Elias had never smoked. He took the photograph from beside the bunk: Mairi on the landing in a summer dress, squinting into the sun, one hand on the pram. He left the framed print of the lighthouse under snow. It belonged to the room, and no one in the cottage needed reminding what the tower looked like.

By three the rain had passed east. Elias made his final round with a rag and oil can. On the stair below the lantern, he found a moth turning helplessly against the glass and carried it down in his cupped hand.

Outside, the sky had cleared. The gannet was gone from the northern rocks.

He released the moth, which fell toward the grass and vanished.

Before dawn, Elias sat at the office desk and completed the log. The words came in their usual boxes. Wind. Sea. Pressure. Lamp pressure maintained. He reached the space for remarks and rested the pen there until a drop of ink swelled at the point.

At 0612 observed trawler heading north-northeast, he wrote.

The horizon lightened. He climbed the tower once more.

Day collected slowly in the lantern panes. The beam became pale on the water, then invisible, though the lens continued its turning and the flame still burned. Elias waited until the sun’s rim appeared beneath a bank of cloud.

He stopped the mechanism. The lens came to rest with one bright panel facing inland.

In the service room, the new cabinet hummed. Elias followed the six numbered steps. A green lamp illuminated behind a round glass eye. Somewhere inside, a relay made a sound like a latch lifting.

He returned to the lantern room and lowered the old flame. It shrank from white to yellow, from yellow to a blue bead. For an instant it seemed to steady itself. Then it went out.

Below, the morning launch rounded the point, small and punctual.

Elias took his case and locked the quarters. On the landing he found a short length of clear fishing line clinging to his coat sleeve. He drew it free. It shone between his fingers, catching the sun, and when he opened his hand the wind carried it out over the water.
