The engineers had left the new panel uncovered, and its small green light watched Ambrose from across the service room like something patient.

He did not look at it while he made his tea. He had learned, in fifty-one years of tending the light, that some things were best approached sideways. You watched the sea by not watching it. You noticed the weather in the set of a gull's wings before the barometer bothered to agree. So he let the kettle tick and hiss on the ring, and he set two spoons of leaves in the brown pot, though he had drunk alone since Meg died, and he did not look at the green light.

Outside, the wind came up off the water the way it always did after sunset, feeling along the tower's flanks for a way in. The glass in the lantern room hummed with it. Ambrose knew the note. He knew all the tower's notes, the way a man knows the breathing of someone asleep beside him.

He carried his cup up the spiral stair. One hundred and thirty-two steps. His knees counted them now in a way they had not when he was young, when he had gone up two at a time with a full scuttle of oil in each hand and his father's voice coming down behind him, telling him to mind the trim, mind the wick, a light that flickers is a lie told to a ship.

The lamp was already lit. That was the strange thing, the thing he could not get his hands to believe. He had not lit it. The mechanism had. At four forty-one it had woken by itself and thrown the beam out across the water, and it would put itself to sleep at dawn, and it would do this tomorrow and the day after and every day after that with no man on the stair at all.

He stood in the lantern room and watched the beam sweep. Out, and dark, and out again. The great lens turned on its bath of mercury, silent, enormous, a thing of a thousand glass facets that his grandfather's grandfather might have polished. Ambrose had polished it that morning. He had not been able to help himself. He had gone up with the chamois and the whiting and done every panel, working the cloth in slow circles, and when he finished he had stood with his hand flat against the brass and felt the turning come up through his palm, patient and endless, like the pulse of some animal too large to fear him.

"There," he had said to it. He did not know why.

Now he set his tea on the sill and took out his pipe and did not light it. The tobacco tin was nearly empty. He had not bought more. There seemed no sense in laying in stores for a house he would leave in the morning, and yet the empty tin troubled him more than he could say, as though a full one might have argued his case.

The relief boat would come at nine. A young man named Pryce would be on it, sent up from the board's office, cheerful and sorry in equal measure, the way the young were when they carried bad news they had not made. Pryce had been up twice already to see to the automation. He called the new panel "the gear" and spoke to Ambrose slowly, and once, meaning kindly, had said that it must be a great weight off, at his age, not to have the responsibility.

Ambrose had said nothing to that. What was there to say. That the weight was the thing itself. That a man carried a weight so long it became the shape of him, and take it away and you did not free him, you only left him with the ache of the place where it had been.

He looked down through the streaming glass at the water. The light found the tops of the waves and left them, found them and left them. Somewhere out past the reef a small craft was working north, its own lights faint, and Ambrose watched it the way he had watched ten thousand others, feeling the old reach of it, the invisible line that went out from the tower to the boat and said, here is the rock, here is the danger, I am awake, go safely, I have you.

The boat would never know the difference now. That was what the engineers did not understand, or understood and thought did not matter. The light would shine the same. The beam did not care whose hand had cleaned the lens. To the sailor cold at his wheel it would be the same light, faithful, turning, and he would bless it and never know that no one blessed him back.

Ambrose thought that this, in the end, was the whole of it. For fifty-one years a man had stood here and cared. Not tended only. Cared. Had lain awake in the small hours listening to the wind and thinking of the boats out in it. Had felt each one as a thread pulling faintly at his chest. And now the caring was to be taken out like a worn part and thrown away, and a green light would sit in the service room and feel nothing, and the ships would be just as safe, and no one, not one soul the length of the coast, would be the poorer for it.

Except him. He would be the poorer.

He found that he was not angry. He had been angry, back in the spring, when the letter came. But the anger had gone out of him the way weather goes, and left this clean washed quietness behind, and in the quietness he could see the thing plainly. The world did not owe him his usefulness. The sea did not. He had been given fifty-one years of mattering to something larger than himself, of being the wakeful mind inside the stone, and that was more than most men got, and it was ending, and both of those were simply true.

He drank his tea. It had gone cool. He drank it anyway.

Below, faintly, he heard the telephone in the service room, the board ringing up to confirm the morning's arrangements, and he did not go down to answer it. Let it ring. Let the green light answer it, if it was so clever. He smiled a little at that, and the smile surprised him, and he was glad no one was there to see it and mistake it for bitterness, because it was not bitter. It was only old.

The night deepened. The beam went out and out across the black water. Ambrose stayed by the glass long after his legs told him to sit, because it seemed to him that a thing ought to be seen off properly, the way you sat up with the dying, not because your sitting changed anything but because to leave would be a kind of lie. He had told the ships the truth for fifty-one years. He would not begin the last night with a lie, even a small one, even one only he would know.

Toward dawn the wind fell away. The sea went from black to grey to the colour of old pewter, and the beam grew thin against the coming light, and at last, at the appointed minute, the lamp put itself out. Ambrose watched it do it. The great lens slowed and stopped. The turning came up through the floor into his feet, and then it did not.

He stood a while in the silence. Then he laid his hand flat against the cold brass, the way he had that morning, the way his whole life had taught his hand to do, and he left it there until the boat's horn sounded far below, small and cheerful across the flat water, calling him down.

"There," he said.

And he went down the hundred and thirty-two steps, counting them, into the morning.
