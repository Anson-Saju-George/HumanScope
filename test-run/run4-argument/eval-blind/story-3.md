# Literary Prizes Should Reward Authors, Not Text Generators

A literary prize is not merely a quality-control sticker placed on an attractive arrangement of words. It is a public judgment about achievement: this writer made this work, and the making deserves recognition. That premise is so ordinary that prize rules rarely bother to defend it. The Booker calls its winner the best eligible work of long-form fiction, but the money, publicity, and altered career belong to the author. Alfred Nobel's will is plainer still: the literature prize goes to “the person” who produced the outstanding work. If a generative model supplies the fiction’s substantial expressive language or narrative decisions, the resulting book should not be eligible for a conventional literary prize. Human-authored fiction should remain eligible when AI serves only as an assistive tool, provided that use is disclosed. The line will sometimes be disputed, but difficulty at the boundary is no reason to abandon the category of authorship at the center.

This position does not depend on claiming that machines can produce only bad sentences. They can produce fluent, affecting, and occasionally startling ones. Nor does it require a mystical account of creativity. It rests on the purpose of the institution. A literary award recognizes choices sustained across a work: which consciousness to inhabit, what pressure to place on a sentence, when to withhold information, what moral cost an ending should refuse to settle. Readers encounter those choices in the text, but a prize attributes them to a maker. That attribution is why a win can establish a career rather than simply raise a book’s consumer rating. When the decisive expressive choices are generated by a system and merely selected by an entrant, the prize would be crediting the wrong agent.

The distinction between tool use and delegated authorship is familiar outside AI. A novelist may use spellcheck, dictate because typing is inaccessible, consult an editor, search an archive, or ask friends how a scene lands. These aids do not ordinarily originate the work’s sentences and structure in the novelist’s place. Generative AI can also be used within that supporting role: to organize notes, identify continuity errors, or offer criticism that the writer accepts or rejects. Banning every contact with it would confuse assistance with substitution and could penalize disabled writers or writers working across languages. The relevant question is not whether software touched the process. It is whether the named author determined the work’s expressive elements.

That standard has a useful real-world analogue. In its 2025 report on AI and copyrightability, the U.S. Copyright Office concluded that AI-assisted work can still receive protection when a human has determined sufficient expressive elements, while mere prompting does not make the user the author of the output. Copyright law and prize rules serve different purposes, but the distinction is apt: instruction is not execution. “Write a restrained scene in which two sisters discover their father’s betrayal” may specify a desired result; it does not choose the diction, rhythm, images, evasions, and turns by which the scene becomes itself. Selecting the best of twenty generated versions adds judgment, as choosing a winning submission does, but a judge does not thereby become its author.

Some will answer that only the finished book matters. On this view, asking how a novel was made is philistinism: if the pages are excellent, reward them. That argument mistakes one kind of evaluation for every kind. We can judge a sunset beautiful without imagining that it deserves a fellowship. We can admire an anonymous folktale without awarding its collector a prize for composing it. Literary prizes routinely impose conditions unrelated to the isolated text—publication dates, language, residency, citizenship, whether the author is living—because they are institutions with particular constituencies and purposes. The 2026 Prime Minister’s Literary Awards in Australia, for example, require eligible entries to be the work of their named creators and exclude books generated wholly by AI. Such a rule does not deny that an excluded object might be readable. It says that this particular honor is for human literary achievement.

The authorship requirement also protects the ecology that makes prizes meaningful. Generative systems radically reduce the cost of producing plausible submissions. In February 2023, *Clarkesworld* temporarily closed submissions after receiving 500 machine-written stories alongside 700 legitimate ones that month. The episode was not a referendum on whether any AI output could be good; it revealed an asymmetry. A writer may spend years on a novel, while an opportunist can produce and enter variations at industrial speed. Prize judging is scarce human attention. If unlimited generated works compete in the same pool, the predictable result is not a purified meritocracy but a screening burden that favors publishers and entrants with the greatest capacity to flood, filter, and market. Human work becomes harder to find precisely because machine production is cheap.

Exclusion should not, however, become technological puritanism. The Children’s Booker Prize rules for 2027 declare books containing any AI-written text ineligible. That bright line is easy to administer in theory, but it is too blunt as a general model. A novel might quote a chatbot because the exchange is part of its subject, much as it might incorporate a form letter, an advertisement, or found language. Rie Qudan’s *Sympathy Tower Tokyo*, winner of the 2024 Akutagawa Prize, reportedly used chatbot language for roughly five percent of a novel centrally concerned with AI and language. Treating that bounded, artistically framed material as equivalent to outsourcing a manuscript erases the very human choice that gives quotation its meaning. Collage, appropriation, and procedural constraint have long belonged to literature. The question is who designed and controlled the work, not whether every character was typed from an unaided imagination.

A workable policy should therefore require entrants to warrant that the named author is responsible for the work’s substantial expressive content and disclose any generative AI incorporated into the submitted text. Administrators could request relevant drafts or process records when credible concerns arise, just as they investigate plagiarism or eligibility disputes. Prompting a model to draft scenes, chapters, or prose that remains substantially intact should make a work ineligible. Using a model for research leads, brainstorming, proofreading, accessibility, or feedback should not. Limited generated language may be permissible when it is clearly disclosed and subordinated to a human artistic design—quotation rather than ghostwriting. Deception about any of this should itself be grounds for disqualification.

No percentage can settle every case. Ten generated words could contain the line on which an entire story turns; ten generated pages could be transformed beyond recognition by a writer’s revision. Committees will need judgment, and some decisions will be controversial. Yet literary prizes already judge originality, translation, collaboration, and suspected borrowing without pretending that a word-count formula can replace deliberation. An imperfect but intelligible authorship standard is better than either extreme: a ban so broad that it treats spellcheck as cheating, or an “anything goes” rule that converts curation into composition.

Fully AI-generated fiction may deserve exhibitions, experiments, criticism, or competitions designed for computational art. Those venues could credit model developers, system designers, dataset curators, and prompt authors honestly instead of compressing a distributed technical production into the familiar fiction of a lone novelist. What it should not receive is a prize whose cultural force depends on recognizing a human writer’s accomplishment. Literary prizes do more than rank texts. They decide whom a culture will read, pay, teach, translate, and remember. As long as that is their function, authorship is not an obsolete sentiment attached to the work. It is the achievement being awarded.
