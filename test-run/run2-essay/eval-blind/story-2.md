# The Blue Cup

*A fictional personal essay*

The last thing my mother asked me was whether I had found a place to park.

She had been in St. Vincent’s for four days by then, long enough for me to know which elevator shuddered between the third and fourth floors and which vending machine sometimes dropped two packets of peanut butter crackers. Long enough for a paper cup of coffee to cost less in my mind than the walk downstairs to get it. Parking had become its own illness: the circling, the ticket that vanished into the seam beside the handbrake, the yellow level that looked green under fluorescent lights.

“On the roof,” I said. “I’m practically in a cloud.”

“You’ll get rained on.”

“It isn’t raining.”

She looked toward the window as if the weather might be different on her side of the glass. It was late October. Beyond the hospital, the city had gone the color of a spoon.

“It will,” she said.

I had brought her the blue cup from home. The nurses gave her water in a pink plastic pitcher with a lid that never sat straight, and she disliked drinking from Styrofoam. The blue cup was thick pottery, white inside, with a chip along the rim. She had bought it years earlier at a church sale, though for some reason she always said she had “won” it. At breakfast she used it for tea. At night she set her dentures in it. This seemed to me an argument against sentiment, but the cup had been on our kitchen shelf through three refrigerators and two remodels, so sentiment had already won.

She lifted it with both hands, took a swallow, and made a face.

“Warm.”

“I just filled it.”

“The pipes in this place must run through hell.”

Her voice had returned that morning. The day before, breathing had occupied her too completely for conversation. Now the oxygen tube rested under her nose, and the numbers on the monitor had steadied into a pattern I allowed myself to misunderstand. A respiratory therapist had said, “We like what we’re seeing.” The sentence became a small room I could stand in.

I emptied the cup in the bathroom and let the tap run. When I brought it back, she held it against her cheek.

“That’s better.”

On the television, a woman in a red jacket pointed to a map covered in rain. My mother watched with the suspicion she usually reserved for contractors. She had lived in the same county for seventy-one years and believed weather reports were a form of boasting.

“How’s your brother?” she asked.

“Fine.”

“That means you haven’t called him.”

“It means he’s fine.”

“Call him.”

“You call him.”

“I’m busy.”

She tipped her head toward the machines, pleased with herself. I laughed, and she did too, though laughing made her cough. I reached for the nurse button. She waved me off, one sharp movement of the fingers, and waited for the coughing to pass.

There are conversations that announce themselves. They happen at restaurant tables after one person says, “We need to talk,” or in parked cars with the engine off. They make a clearing around themselves. You understand that the words will be kept, that even the saltshaker may later become evidence. This was not one of those conversations. I had my coat on. I had left my bag zipped. Every few minutes I checked the time on my phone, not rudely, I thought, but visibly.

My mother asked if I had paid the water bill at her house. I said yes. She asked whether I had used a stamp from the roll in the second drawer.

“I paid it online.”

“They charge you for that.”

“They don’t.”

“They will.”

She told me there was chicken in the freezer, wrapped in butcher paper, not the foil package, which was liver. She said the porch light needed a bulb. She asked me to take the library books back before Tuesday, because she would not pay another fine to people whose salaries came out of her taxes.

I put these instructions into the notes app on my phone. CHICKEN—PAPER. PORCH BULB. LIBRARY. The list survives because I never delete anything. It sits between a password for a streaming service I no longer use and the dimensions of a window I never curtained.

At some point a nurse came in to check the IV. My mother asked her name again, and when the nurse said “Maribel,” my mother repeated it carefully. She had always done that with names, giving each syllable back to its owner. Maribel said my mother might move out of intensive care the next day if she kept improving.

“See?” my mother said after she left. “I’m being promoted.”

I said I would bring her robe. She wanted the green one, not the fuzzy one that made sparks when she pulled it over her head. She wanted her own toothpaste. She wanted the crossword page from Sunday’s paper, though Sunday was four days gone and the recycling had already been collected.

“I can get you another crossword.”

“It was a good one.”

“How do you know if you didn’t do it?”

“I looked at it.”

This was the closest we came to an argument. I said I would see what I could find. She said not to go digging through the recycling on her account. I said I wouldn’t. We both knew I might.

When I stood to leave, she inspected me in the way mothers can, as if I were a room she had cleaned once and someone else had let go. She told me my sweater was pilling under the arms. I told her nobody looked under my arms. She said that was no reason to abandon standards.

Then she asked about the parking.

On the roof, it had begun to rain. Water glazed the concrete, and the city lights spread across it in long, trembling bars. I ran the last few yards to the car, laughing because she had been right, because she was so predictably right that it felt like another stable number on a screen.

At home I called my brother. I returned neither the library books nor the call that came from the hospital at 2:17 in the morning. My phone was charging in the kitchen with the sound turned off. By the time I woke, there were three messages.

For weeks afterward, people asked whether we had talked. They meant: Had she known I loved her? Had she been afraid? Had she left me some final sentence I could unfold for the rest of my life? I said yes, we had talked. Usually that was enough.

The blue cup came home in a clear plastic bag with her comb, her glasses, and the green robe I had brought too late. Someone had washed it. I put it in the cupboard above my sink, behind the glasses I actually used.

That winter, I remembered the list. The library had waived the fines after I explained. The porch light was replaced by then. My brother had taken the chicken, or perhaps the liver; we never settled which. I opened the note on my phone and stood with my thumb over the trash icon. Then I added one more line: BLUE CUP.

I still drink from it sometimes. I avoid the chipped place without looking for it. When rain is forecast, I carry an umbrella to the car, even if the sky is clear.
