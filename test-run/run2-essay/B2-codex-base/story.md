# The Forecast

The last thing my father said to me was, “Bring a sweater.”

It was July, and I was standing in my parents’ kitchen with one hand on the screen door. Outside, the afternoon had gone white with heat. Cicadas screamed from the maples. My father sat at the table in a sleeveless undershirt, a glass of iced tea sweating onto the crossword, and still he looked past me toward the driveway as if he could see weather I could not.

“Dad,” I said. “It’s ninety-two degrees.”

“Gets cold by the water.”

I was driving three hours north to spend the weekend at a friend’s cottage. I had packed badly and late, as I did most things then, and my father had watched me make three trips between the house and my dented Honda: duffel bag, grocery sack, pillow. Each time I passed through the kitchen, he offered another item I had forgotten. Flashlight. Bug spray. Quarters for the toll. I refused each one with the theatrical patience of a twenty-six-year-old who believes competence consists mainly of rejecting help.

“I’ll be fine,” I said.

He lifted one shoulder. “Suit yourself.”

That was our final conversation. Not a confession, not a blessing, not one of those polished exchanges that stories arrange beside a deathbed. A weather report and a minor disagreement. I let the screen door clap shut behind me and did not turn around.

For years, I remembered the moment incorrectly. In my first version, my father looked tired. In the next, he pressed a hand to his chest. Later, I supplied a tremor in his voice, some detectable warning that I had missed through selfishness or haste. Memory, embarrassed by the ordinary, kept trying to make the scene worthy of what came after.

What came after was a phone call at 6:14 the next morning. My mother’s voice was so controlled that, for several seconds, I thought she was calling about the cat. Then I was barefoot on a splintered dock while my friend searched for my keys. The lake lay flat and indifferent, holding the pink reflection of the sky. My father had collapsed before dawn. By the time the ambulance arrived, he was gone.

On the drive home, I replayed our last exchange until the words lost meaning. Bring a sweater. It’s ninety-two degrees. Gets cold by the water. I’ll be fine. Suit yourself. Five lines, none of them equal to a life. I wanted to return to the kitchen and force us into eloquence. I would put down my keys. I would sit across from him. We would speak honestly about his childhood, my future, the stubborn tenderness that passed between us disguised as irritation. Before leaving, I would say, “I love you,” and he would answer without joking.

Instead, I had said, “I’ll be fine,” which now sounded less like reassurance than betrayal.

My father was not a man who made love easy to quote. He changed the oil before the warning light came on. He sharpened our kitchen knives and left them lined on a towel, gleaming and suddenly dangerous. When I moved into my first apartment, he drove two hours to install a deadbolt, then complained about the screws that came with it. Praise from him arrived sideways: “You didn’t embarrass yourself,” he told me after my college graduation. Concern took the form of an interrogation about tire pressure.

As a child, I found this language perfectly clear. I brought him broken toys and waited beside his workbench as he opened them. I knew the lamp left on in the hallway meant he was awake until I came home. Only after I grew older did I begin demanding translation. Other families ended phone calls with easy declarations. Other fathers gave advice about love and work. Mine asked whether I had checked the antifreeze. I mistook the limits of his vocabulary for the limits of his feeling.

After the funeral, the kitchen filled with casseroles. Neighbors sat in his chair and told stories in softened voices. I kept seeing his unfinished crossword beneath the iced-tea ring, though my mother had already thrown the newspaper away. Grief made every object accusatory. The flashlight was in the junk drawer, its batteries wrapped in a rubber band. The bug spray stood on a shelf in the garage. A gray cardigan hung behind the mudroom door.

That sweater became, for a while, the center of the tragedy. If I had gone back for it, I imagined, we might have spoken longer. Perhaps he would have mentioned feeling unwell. Perhaps I would have noticed something. Perhaps ten additional minutes could have changed the machinery of his heart. Grief is skilled at turning love into evidence. It builds a courtroom inside you, appoints you prosecutor and defendant, and calls the dead—who cannot correct the record—as its only witness.

My mother finally interrupted the trial. Months later, while we were sorting his clothes, I confessed what I had been thinking. She listened with one of his flannel shirts folded across her lap.

“He told everybody to bring a sweater,” she said.

I laughed, which felt briefly like dropping something breakable.

“You could’ve been going to the equator,” she continued. “He’d have worried about air-conditioning.”

Then she told me that, after I left, he had finished his tea, complained about the baseball game, and gone to bed. He had not seemed frightened or sick. There was no secret message in his warning, no premonition. He had simply been my father until the moment he wasn’t.

I took the gray cardigan home.

For a long time it hung in my closet untouched. It smelled first like him—sawdust, soap, the faint mineral scent of the garage—and then, gradually, like nothing. I resented the fading. I had believed grief would preserve what mattered, but grief is not a museum. It is weather. It changes the shape of things, wears down their sharp edges, moves in and out without asking.

Years later, I wore the cardigan to the hospital when my daughter was born. The maternity ward was over-air-conditioned, and sometime after midnight I pulled it around my shoulders. My wife slept. Our daughter lay in a clear plastic bassinet, making small astonished noises. I reached through the opening and placed one finger in her fist.

“You’re cold,” I whispered, although she was not.

In that instant, my father’s last words changed. They were no longer a failed farewell. They belonged to the long conversation we had been having all my life, one conducted through packed lunches, tightened bolts, porch lights, and forecasts. Bring a sweater meant I have imagined you arriving after the temperature drops. It meant some part of me is traveling with you. It meant I cannot control the weather, the road, the hidden weakness in my own heart, but let me offer this small protection.

I had spent years grieving that he did not know our conversation was the last. Now I think there was mercy in his ignorance. Had he known, terror would have entered the room. We might have reached for grand words and found them unnatural in our mouths. Instead, he spoke fluently. I answered in the dialect of the young—I’ll be fine—and he let me go.

My daughter is old enough now to roll her eyes when I remind her to take a jacket. She stands at the door, half inside her own life already, while I inventory the clouds. Sometimes I hear my father’s voice emerge from mine, practical and unadorned. I want to stop her, explain the inheritance, tell her that warnings are one of the forms love takes when it is afraid to name itself.

But she is in a hurry. So I hold out the sweater. She sighs and takes it. The door closes, and for a moment I stand listening on the other side, grateful for the ordinary words we have been given, none of us knowing which ones will have to last.
