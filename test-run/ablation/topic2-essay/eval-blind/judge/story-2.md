The last time I spoke to my grandfather, we argued about a bird feeder.

I want to say we talked about something worthy of a final conversation. That we traded some inheritance of wisdom, that he told me what he had learned in eighty-one years and I received it the way you're supposed to, with both hands. But that isn't what happened. What happened is that he had bought a squirrel-proof feeder from a catalog, the kind with a spring-loaded perch that drops the weight of anything heavier than a chickadee, and he was convinced it didn't work, and I was convinced he had assembled it wrong. We went back and forth on the phone for maybe twenty minutes. He kept describing the mechanism. I kept saying, Grandpa, read me the instructions. He said he'd thrown them out. Of course he had.

This was a Tuesday. I remember because I was folding laundry, and I had the phone pinched between my ear and shoulder, and I was mostly listening to the part of him that liked to complain, which was a large and well-developed part, and I was giving him about sixty percent of my attention. The other forty percent was on a fitted sheet, which as everyone knows cannot be folded and can only be wrestled into a shape you agree to stop caring about. So the truth is that during what turned out to be the last conversation of my grandfather's life, I was thinking about a fitted sheet.

I've made a kind of peace with this, but it took a while, and the peace is not the clean kind.

Here is the thing nobody warns you about last conversations: they are almost never last conversations. That's the whole cruelty of the design. A last conversation, by definition, has to be indistinguishable from all the others, or it wouldn't be the last one — it would be a deathbed scene, a held hand, a summoning of the family. Those exist, and people who get them are, in a grim way, lucky, though it's a luck no one would choose. Most last conversations are Tuesdays. Most of them are about bird feeders. You don't rise to the occasion because there is no occasion. There's just your grandfather, being difficult about a spring, and you, being impatient about a spring, and the ordinary friction of two people who love each other and are also slightly annoying to each other, which is what love mostly is once you subtract the ceremony.

He died in his sleep on Thursday. His heart, they said, though I've never trusted how casually that word gets used, as if the heart were a component that simply reaches the end of its warranty. I got the call from my mother in the afternoon. And the first coherent thought I had, after the noise in my head died down, was: the feeder. He never found out whether it worked.

I know how small that is. I knew it then. A man had died and I was thinking about squirrels. But grief doesn't organize itself by importance. It grabs whatever's nearest, and what was nearest was the unfinished argument, the loose thread I'd left dangling, the one problem between us that hadn't been resolved and now never would be. I would have given a great deal, in those first days, to be able to call him back and say: You were right. It's a bad feeder. Send it back. Just to close the loop. Just to let him win one.

Because that's the other thing. In the argument, I had been condescending. Not cruel — I want to be fair to myself, I wasn't cruel — but I'd used the voice you use with an old person who you've decided is confused. Read me the instructions, Grandpa. As if he were a child. As if eighty-one years of him being a competent adult, a man who had wired his own house and kept a checkbook balanced to the penny, could be waved away because he was struggling with one catalog gadget. I heard myself do it. I heard the impatience and I let it stand, because I assumed, the way you always assume, that there would be a next time to be kinder. There is a bank account of future patience we all believe we're saving into. We are not. The account is a fiction. There is only the balance you're spending right now, in this call, on this Tuesday.

For a long time I thought the lesson here was supposed to be about last words — that I should walk around treating every goodbye like it might be the final one, saying I love you with terrible sincerity at the end of every phone call, never hanging up on an unresolved note. People give this advice. It sounds wise. But I've come to think it's not only impossible but wrong, because a life lived as though every conversation were a potential eulogy would be unbearable, airless, false. You cannot bring the full weight of mortality to a discussion about a bird feeder. If you tried, you'd never get anything said. The ordinariness is not a bug. The ordinariness is the gift. It means you were comfortable enough with each other to be petty, to bicker about a spring, to not perform.

What I actually took from it is quieter and harder to hold onto. It's not that I should have said something profound. It's that I could have been softer inside the unprofound thing. I didn't need a better subject. I needed a better tone. I could have laughed instead of sighed. I could have said, You know what, send me a picture of the thing and I'll figure it out, which would have cost me nothing and would have told him he was worth my forty percent. The content of a last conversation is out of your hands — you don't get to choose the topic, because you don't know it's the last one. But the warmth is always in your hands. The warmth is the only part you control.

I did, eventually, buy the same feeder. I'm aware of how this sounds. I set it up on the maple behind my house and I watched it for a week, and I want to report that it worked perfectly, that he'd been wrong, that I could feel vindicated on his behalf and finally let it go. But that's not what happened either. A squirrel figured it out in about a day and a half. It learned to hang upside down from the roof of the thing and reach the seed from an angle the spring didn't account for. It sat there eating, completely unbothered by the mechanism designed to defeat it, and I stood at my kitchen window and laughed until I wasn't laughing anymore.

He was right. It's a bad feeder.

I never got to tell him. That's the last conversation now, the one I have alone, at the window, every time the squirrel comes back — which is daily, and shameless, and somehow exactly what I want.
