Higher is better on all four scores, including **generic/formulaic: 5 means least formulaic**. All six are reasonably close to 600 words. Shared materials—brass, tea, logbooks—aren’t automatically clichés; what matters is what each story does with them.

**T1, “Remarks” — Overall rank: 1.** The strongest because its final action changes the significance of the logbook and reveals something consequential about Tomas.

- **Generic/formulaic: 4/5.** The weakest phrases are “his hands wanted a job” and “where the next person would find it if there ever was one.” Both are familiar shorthand for occupational displacement. The obsolete mantles wrapped in an extinct newspaper also stack the obsolescence rather deliberately. But the story usually makes its details serve this particular man.
- **Craft: 5/5.** Dessie arguing about diesel, and Tomas choosing not to approach him, establish a relationship more effectively than a paragraph declaring their unspoken bond would. The historical log entries prepare the ending without all pointing toward one prescribed interpretation. The pacing gives the actual signal exchange room to matter.
- **Emotional effect/meaning: 5/5.** “Returned signal” is excellent: Tomas records a response nobody could have seen, claiming that his gesture counted. His long failure to answer makes this more than a routine farewell. The uncertainty about Dessie’s intentions works because Tomas still acts; uncertainty itself isn’t the achievement. The final business with the pen slightly dilutes an ending already achieved.
- **Brief fit: 4/5.** Approximately 667 words. Strong focus on the final watch, but automation is already operating alongside him that evening, rather than beginning afterward.

**T2, “The Last Watch” — Overall rank: 2.** The most sustained treatment of the practical differences between maintaining a light and attending to a place.

- **Generic/formulaic: 4/5.** “It looked the same as it always had. He had half expected it to look different” is a familiar, underdeveloped displacement beat. “He wasn’t sure anymore who it was for” explains a question the logbook scene has already raised. Otherwise, the details generally resist becoming mere retirement-story furnishings.
- **Craft: 4/5.** Buying his own brass polish and questioning quarterly cleaning give Owen professional standards, not just attachment. The parenthetical “automatic” changes his relationship to his own habitual sentence. One weakness: his attempt to beat the sensor at dawn is awkward after the switch has been described as one “that no longer did anything.” A knowingly symbolic gesture is possible, but the narration treats it rather like a practical contest.
- **Emotional effect/meaning: 4/5.** The gannet arrives conveniently, but caring for it gives his presence a concrete consequence beyond keeping the machinery running. That earns feeling. The blank relief-signature line also makes absence specific. Neither moment is startling, and the bird episode is compressed, but both belong to the story’s conception of his work.
- **Brief fit: 4/5.** Approximately 635 words. A complete final watch, although the light is automated before the night begins.

**T3, “The Last Watch” — Overall rank: 4.** A competent, occasionally touching story whose familiar emotional equipment limits its individuality.

- **Generic/formulaic: 3/5.** The clearest offenders are “He knew the pattern the way he knew his own pulse,” “It never had, not on his watch,” and “He still set out two cups some mornings before he remembered.” These supply intimacy, professional pride, and bereavement through readily available formulas. The two cups are especially conventional.
- **Craft: 3/5.** The face reflected “like a man at the bottom of a spoon” is a good physical observation. The father-in-law’s handwriting gives the station a family history, and “M. brought up soup” brings that history briefly alive. But much of the middle is a procession through evocative objects and memories; the trawler’s passage generates little new pressure or choice.
- **Emotional effect/meaning: 3/5.** The final supplementary entry preserves the keeper’s attention to things outside the official record. That is coherent and mildly moving. However, the imagined future reader wondering about soup has already explained why these details matter, so the ending mostly confirms an established proposition. The soup carries more feeling than the explicit bereavement material.
- **Brief fit: 5/5.** Approximately 672 words. The stated handover at dawn supplies the requested final night before automation takes over.

**T4, “The Last Watch” — Overall rank: 6.** The clearest literal fit, but the story most dependent on assigning sentimental meaning to familiar props.

- **Generic/formulaic: 2/5.** “His wife had called it the tower’s heartbeat,” “He wanted to feel the building answer,” and “His hand was steady, which surprised him” all arrive with their emotional interpretation attached. The dead wife, aching knee, redundant cleaning, and abandoned cloth accumulate predictably.
- **Craft: 3/5.** The knee’s particular stopping points establish a bodily rhythm, and the longstanding violation of the tea rule gives Tomas a small personal habit. The trawler making its turn regardless of his watching is also effective. But the imagined engineer wondering “why a machine would need a cloth” feels forced: an engineer would understand that equipment needs cleaning. The closing image depends on making the future artificially uncomprehending.
- **Emotional effect/meaning: 2/5.** The final pleasure in his aching knee is prepared by the opening, but still feels like an assigned consolation: pain confirms a continuity the machinery cannot remove. The night hasn’t developed that feeling enough to make the reversal persuasive. Similarly, the cloth is presented as poignant without acquiring much personal significance beyond its obvious function.
- **Brief fit: 5/5.** Approximately 631 words. Automation will be installed the following noon, so this is unequivocally the last night beforehand.

**T5, “The Last Watch at Carrick Rock” — Overall rank: 3.** Several excellent character actions survive an unnecessarily emphatic statement of the lesson.

- **Generic/formulaic: 3/5.** The worst passage is “he understood that this was the whole point, and had always been the point, and that the point had never been him.” That is the tidy epiphany, complete with repetition to ensure reception. “The cabinet did not look at him” also strains to make a machine’s indifference meaningful.
- **Craft: 4/5.** Hugh hearing the generator change before the cabinet responds gives him demonstrated expertise and complicates Priest’s assurance. Standing in his socks supplies vulnerability without naming it. Best of all, he washes his cup, puts it away, then retrieves it for his bag: an ordinary action becomes an actual decision about leaving.
- **Emotional effect/meaning: 4/5.** The puffin is conspicuously planted for its return, but the return works. Hugh adds an unnecessary personal mark to an institutional record, joining an earlier keeper in something other than duty. “Not very well” helps make the act his own. The ending offers a more interesting response to retirement than the earlier assertion that the work’s point was never him.
- **Brief fit: 4/5.** Approximately 628 words. It concerns his final watch, but the light has already been automated when the story opens.

**T6, “The Last Watch” — Overall rank: 5.** Some strong sensory writing, weakened by repeated explanations and a thinly established farewell.

- **Generic/formulaic: 2/5.** “There was no need,” “Nobody needed Tomas in the lantern room,” and the closing “It didn’t need him to” repeatedly deliver the same conclusion. “His hands were not quite steady” supplies a standard emotional cue. The deceased wife and phantom wick-trimming add further familiar signals of loss.
- **Craft: 3/5.** The changed smell of the lantern room makes the replacement physically perceptible. The LED array “like a coin dropped into a chandelier” is a striking image of scale and incongruity; ornament earns its place there. But the fisherman enters late and remains interchangeable among possible acquaintances. The story gives more space to explaining redundancy than to establishing the relationship that supplies its climax.
- **Emotional effect/meaning: 3/5.** The reciprocal flashes offer credible warmth, and “Saw a friend” usefully breaks the official register. But little particular history supports that moment. “For thirty-one years his job had been to make sure nobody noticed him, and the machine would do it better” has already delivered the interpretation, while the final sentence repeats it after the human exchange has opened something more interesting.
- **Brief fit: 4/5.** Approximately 617 words. The final night is central, but automation begins at dusk during that night.

Bluntly, these stories share a remarkably narrow design: departing engineers, repeated chores, tea, a logbook, maritime observation, a final gesture. The best turn that design into choices with consequences: Tomas records an invisible response; Owen tends an injured bird; Hugh takes his cup and draws badly. The weakest expect dead wives, aching knees, historical dates, and abandoned cleaning equipment to arrive already charged with significance. T1 wins because its ending changes our understanding of what happened. T4 mostly labels familiar things as poignant. T5’s explicit epiphany is a weakness because it simplifies the more complicated behavior around it—not because explanation is inherently inferior to suggestion.
