The gulls left Corbie Rock before Fenn did, which he thought was only fair. They had always known ahead of him when a thing was finished.

He came up the ninety-eight steps at half past six with the paraffin can, the way he had come up them for thirty-one years, and did not count them, because a man who counts his own stairs has begun saying goodbye too soon. In the service room the crate was where the boat had left it that afternoon, roped and stencilled GEC — SUNVIC, and he stepped around it as he would step around a sleeping dog. Tomorrow two men in oilskins would open it and teach the tower to work without him. Tonight the tower was still his, and it wanted lighting.

He wound the clock first. The weight hung down the centre of the tower on its wire, and the drum took the handle the way it always had, stiff for the first turns and then easy, and he felt the old machine gather its patience above him, ready to push the great lens round and round through the dark. The lens floated on its bath of mercury, a ton of glass turning on a skin of metal you could dimple with a fingernail. He had shown that trick to a schoolteacher once, years back — pressed the silver with his thumb and let her watch the whole apparatus swing at the touch — and she had written to the Commissioners to say it was the finest thing she had seen in Scotland. He had kept the letter. He did not know now where.

The lamp took three matches. The first two he wasted because his hands were cold, and he said nothing to himself about it. When the mantle caught it went from grey rag to a small hard sun, and he brought the burner up slow so the silk would not tear, and closed the door on it, and stepped back. Above him the beam found the panes and threw itself out to sea, and the room filled and emptied of light, filled and emptied, four seconds and then dark, four seconds and then dark, the way it had told ships who it was since before his father was born. Corbie flashing white every four. Any man off Ardnave with a chart could read the tower's name in that. Tomorrow it would say the same thing to nobody, or to a machine on the mainland that logged the saying.

He went down to the room below and wrote the log. Wind SW 4, falling. Sky clearing after rain. Visibility good. He held the pen over the last of the line the way he held it every night, deciding what the weather had meant, and then wrote nothing further, because the weather had not meant anything, it had only been the weather, and thirty-one years of pretending otherwise did not oblige him to start tonight.

There was tea. He drank it at the desk with his coat on. The light went round overhead and laid its beam across the wall at its four counts, and he did not look up at it, the way you do not look at someone you have lived with a long time to know they are in the room.

It was the loneliness the mainland people always asked about, when they asked. They said it kindly, leaning in, wanting him to give them a shiver they could take home. He never had the shiver to give. A man on a rock with a fire to keep is the least lonely man there is; he is wanted every hour of the night, and the sea will let him know at once if he stops being wanted. It was the wives who were lonely, in the towns, waiting. His had not waited. She had come out to the shore station and then, when he would not leave the rock, had gone in again to Oban, and they had written to each other for a while like two lighthouses on different coasts, flashing across a distance nothing crossed. He did not blame her. He had chosen the rock over her plainly enough, and she had only done the same arithmetic he had, and come to a different answer.

Near eleven he took the round. Trimmed nothing — there was nothing to trim, the mantle was clean — but he went up and stood in the lantern with the great lens turning past his face, the prisms catching him and losing him, catching him and losing him, and he put his hand flat on the brass frame and felt the whole assembly carrying his warmth away into its motion. There was a scratch in the frame at eye height. He had put it there himself in 1951 with a spanner that slipped, and had waited a month to be found out, and never had been, and now the man who would find it could not know what it was, only that the fitting was old and should perhaps be replaced. He looked at the scratch for a while. Then he wound the clock again, though it did not need it yet, because his hands wanted the handle.

The crate he came back to at two, when the tea was cold and the dark was at its thickest and most honest. He read the stencil again. Inside would be the photoelectric cell that watched the sky and decided, at dusk, that it was dusk; and the changer that would swing a fresh lamp into place when one failed, so that no man need climb in the night; and the wire that would carry all of it to a panel on the mainland where a light on a board would burn to say the light on the rock was burning. Clever. He had read the pamphlet twice and found nothing in it to argue with. It would not waste matches. It would not keep a schoolteacher's letter, or scratch a frame, or drink cold tea deciding what the weather meant, and none of that was in the pamphlet's remit, and the pamphlet was right not to mention it.

He thought he might resent them, the two men coming, and found when he looked that he did not. They were keepers of a kind. They would tend a thing that could not tend itself, and go home, and that was the whole of the trade whatever tools it used. He hoped they would be careful with the mercury.

Toward five the sky did what he had watched it do ten thousand times, went from black to the colour of a mussel shell out past Ardnave, and the land came back one grey field at a time. He climbed up and stood by the lamp and waited, not for anything, only until the light out at sea was stronger than the light he was making. There is a moment you learn to feel rather than judge. When it came he reached in and turned the lamp down, and the mantle faded to rag again, and the beam thinned and went, and the lens turned on empty for a revolution before he put the brake to it and stopped the tower, and the great glass came to rest facing the open water, which he had never been able to arrange on purpose and had always liked when it happened.

He wrote the last line of the log. Extinguished 5:14. Then he stood the pen in the well and did not sign under it, because the book would go on after him in another hand, and a signature is a thing you put at the end of a letter, and he had decided the night before that this was not going to be a letter. He wiped the brass rail with his sleeve out of habit, and left the cloth folded on the desk for the men, and went down the ninety-eight steps into the store to wait for the boat, and did not count them going down either.

Outside the gulls were coming back to the rock. They did that too, when the fishing shifted — knew before he did, and came anyway.
