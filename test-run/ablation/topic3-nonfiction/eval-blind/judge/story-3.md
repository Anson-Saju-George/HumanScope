# The Great Boston Molasses Flood of 1919

Just after 12:30 on the afternoon of January 15, 1919, a steel storage tank in Boston's North End tore open and released roughly 2.3 million gallons of molasses into a crowded working-class neighborhood. The dark flood killed 21 people, injured about 150, and flattened homes, a firehouse, and part of an elevated railway. It was not a freak of nature. A badly built tank failed under a load it was never sound enough to hold, and the disaster ended in a landmark legal judgment that helped make professional engineering accountable to the public. That is the short version. The details are worth knowing because they explain why the flood still turns up in engineering ethics courses more than a century later.

## What happened that afternoon

The tank stood on Commercial Street near the harbor, part of a facility run by the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of the United States Industrial Alcohol Company (USIA). It was about 50 feet tall and 90 feet across, and when full it held around 2.3 million gallons of molasses weighing some 13,000 tons. Molasses at the time was a valuable industrial feedstock: distillers fermented and processed it into ethanol, which went into everything from liquor to the munitions that had fueled the First World War, then only two months past its armistice.

When the tank ruptured, witnesses reported a deep rumble and a sound like machine-gun fire as the steel plates ripped apart and the rivets shot loose. A wave of molasses estimated at up to 25 to 40 feet high at its peak surged outward, moving fast enough — accounts put it near 35 miles per hour — that people, horses, and vehicles could not outrun it. The flood swept a firehouse off its foundation, buckled the steel supports of the Boston Elevated Railway, crushed wooden buildings, and pushed debris toward the waterfront. Some victims drowned or suffocated in the viscous flood; others were struck by wreckage. Rescuers waded in waist-deep, and the sticky mass made every movement slow and exhausting. Cleanup crews worked for weeks, using salt water pumped from fireboats to break up the molasses, and the harbor reportedly ran brown for months.

## Why the tank failed

The disaster had two layers of cause: a structure that should never have passed as safe, and the conditions on the day that pushed it past its limit.

The tank was built in 1915, quickly, to meet wartime demand for industrial alcohol. The man who oversaw its construction for USIA, Arthur Jell, had no engineering or architectural training and was under pressure to have the tank ready to receive a shipment of molasses. Crucially, he never had it properly tested. A standard test would have filled the tank with water to check for leaks and confirm the structure could bear a full load; instead, the tank was reportedly filled only a few inches deep with water before being put into service. That shortcut mattered, because the tank leaked from the day it opened. Molasses seeped through seams so visibly that neighborhood children collected it, and the company's response was to paint the tank brown so the leaks would be less obvious rather than to investigate why it was failing.

Later engineering analysis identified deeper flaws. The steel plates were too thin for the loads the tank actually carried, and the design lacked an adequate safety margin against the outward pressure of a full tank. The steel itself was of a type that became brittle in cold weather, low in the manganese that would have made it tougher, so it was prone to cracking rather than flexing. The rivet holes concentrated stress at exactly the points where the plates eventually split. In short, the tank sat far closer to failure, all the time, than anyone using it understood.

Conditions on January 15 supplied the final push. A shipment of warm molasses had been added to the tank a couple of days earlier, on top of colder molasses already inside. Some analyses suggest this raised the internal temperature and may have encouraged fermentation, which produces gas and could have increased pressure inside the tank. The local air temperature had also risen sharply from the days before. None of these conditions would have troubled a properly built tank. Acting on a structure already carrying more stress than its thin, brittle walls could safely bear, they were enough to start the tear that ran away into catastrophe.

A grim detail from the human toll reflects the physics. Molasses is far more viscous than water, and it grows thicker as it cools. Researchers who modeled the flood — including a 2016 study presented by Harvard scientists — noted that the initial wave moved fast enough to be deadly, but that as the molasses spread and cooled in the January air it became harder and harder to move through, trapping people who might otherwise have pulled themselves free. The same substance that let some victims be reached also held others fast.

## The reckoning in court

The legal aftermath is the part that gave the flood its lasting significance. More than 100 lawsuits were consolidated against USIA in one of the earliest large class-action-style proceedings in Massachusetts. The company's central defense was that the tank had not failed on its own but had been destroyed by a bomb — a claim with some surface plausibility in an era of genuine anarchist violence, when mail bombs and dynamite attacks were in the news. If an outside saboteur had blown up the tank, the company could not be held liable for negligence.

The case ran for years before a court-appointed auditor, Hugh W. Ogden, who heard thousands of witnesses and mountains of expert testimony on both sides. In 1925 he ruled against USIA. The weight of the engineering evidence showed that the tank had been structurally inadequate and inadequately tested, and that the company was responsible. USIA ultimately paid settlements reported to total in the neighborhood of 600,000 dollars — a very large sum at the time, equivalent to many millions in today's money — divided among the families of the dead and the injured.

## Why it still matters

The flood matters today for reasons that outlast the North End cleanup.

First, it changed how large structures are approved. The disaster is frequently cited as a spur to stricter oversight of engineering and construction in Massachusetts and beyond, including requirements that structural calculations and plans be prepared, signed, and sealed by a certified professional engineer or architect before a project is built. The core idea — that a named, licensed professional must put their credibility behind a design's safety — is now standard practice, and the molasses tank is a textbook example of what its absence permits.

Second, it is a durable lesson in how disasters actually happen. The tank did not fail because of one villain or one storm. It failed because a rushed schedule, an unqualified overseer, a skipped safety test, cheap and brittle materials, and a habit of hiding warning signs rather than acting on them all lined up. Each visible leak was a signal, and each was painted over. Engineers and safety instructors use the story precisely because that pattern — small ignored warnings compounding into a fatal failure — recurs in modern accidents.

Third, it is a case study in accountability. A powerful company tried to shift the blame to an invisible saboteur, and a patient, evidence-driven inquiry held it responsible anyway. That outcome helped establish that corporations could be made to answer for negligence on a large scale.

The molasses flood is easy to remember as an oddity, a city drowned in syrup. But the reasons it happened, and the reforms that followed, are ordinary and serious. A structure carrying enormous stored energy was trusted to walls no one had honestly checked. When those walls gave way, the cost was measured in lives — and the response reshaped the rules meant to keep it from happening again.
