The last time I spoke to my grandfather, we argued about a bird.

It was a Tuesday, or maybe a Wednesday. I have tried since to fix the day in place, to give it the weight it deserves, but the truth is that it was an ordinary afternoon and I treated it like one. I called him because my mother had asked me to, in that particular tone she used when a request was really an instruction. "He's been low," she said. "Just check in." So I checked in, the way you check a box.

He answered on the fourth ring, breathing a little heavily, which I did not think about at the time. He always answered slowly. He was eighty-six and lived alone in the house he had built with his brother in 1961, a house that still smelled of pipe smoke though he had quit two decades earlier, as if the walls themselves had taken up the habit and refused to give it up on his behalf.

"There's a bird on the feeder," he said, before I could ask how he was. "I want you to tell me what it is."

This was a game we had played since I was small. He kept a pair of binoculars on the windowsill and a dog-eared field guide on the arm of his chair, and he would describe a bird to me in maddening detail while refusing to simply look it up himself. He said the looking-up was cheating. He said a man should be able to hold a thing in his mind long enough to name it.

So he described the bird. Slate-gray back, he said. A rusty wash along the flanks. White belly. A dark cap, like a little executioner's hood. It was flicking its tail, he said, and making a sound like a rusty gate.

"Dark-eyed junco," I said, too quickly. I was standing in my kitchen with a pan heating on the stove and I wanted the call to be short.

"No," he said. "Juncos are winter birds. It's April."

"Then it's a titmouse."

"A titmouse has a crest. This one's smooth-headed."

"Grandpa, I don't know. Send me a picture."

"I can't work the picture thing on this phone," he said, which was true, and which I found irritating in the way that only the small helplessnesses of the people we love can irritate us. I told him it was probably a junco confused about the season. He said I wasn't paying attention. He was right; I wasn't. The pan was smoking now and I turned the burner down and I said, with an edge I still hear when I lie awake, "Well, it's just a bird."

There was a silence on the line. Not a wounded silence, I think, though I have spent a long time trying to read it. Just a pause, the pause of a man deciding whether a thing was worth pursuing. Then he laughed, a dry rasp of a laugh, and said, "Nothing's just a bird. You'll understand that eventually."

We talked for another six or seven minutes. I don't remember most of it. I remember he asked about my job and I gave him the version I gave everyone, the smoothed-over version with the difficult parts sanded off. I remember he told me the tomatoes he'd started from seed were leggy and he wasn't sure they'd take. I remember he said the house was quiet since the Hendersons next door had moved to Arizona. And I remember that when he said goodbye he said it the way he always did, "All right, kiddo," two words that cost him nothing and that I would give a great deal to hear again.

I made my dinner. I ate it in front of the television. I did not think about him again that night, or the next day, and on the third day my mother called and her voice was doing something I had never heard it do, and I knew before she said the words.

His heart, they said. Quick. He'd been found in his chair by the window, the binoculars in his lap.

Here is the thing I have had to sit with. There was no reason for me to know. He did not know. Death did not send a courier ahead to warn us. It was, by every measure available to me at the time, a completely unremarkable phone call, the kind we had a hundred times and would have had a hundred more. And that is precisely the horror and the mercy of it. We are not, mostly, granted last conversations that announce themselves. We are granted Tuesdays. We are granted the bird on the feeder.

For a long time I was furious at myself for the smoking pan, for the "it's just a bird," for the way I had held the call at arm's length like something I might get on my clothes. I rehearsed alternate versions in which I sat down, in which I gave him my whole attention, in which I said something that mattered. I wanted the conversation to have been a proper goodbye, a scene, a summing-up. I wanted to have known.

But I have come, slowly, to a different thought. If I had known, the call would have been a performance. We would both have been reaching for the large words, the ones that buckle under their own weight, and we would have missed each other in the reaching. What we had instead was true. It was an old man and his grandson doing the small, real thing they had always done, which was to look at the world and try to name it together. He was teaching me, one last time, the lesson I was too busy to learn: that attention is the whole of it. That nothing is just a bird.

I bought a field guide, after. I keep it on the arm of my own chair now, and I have hung a feeder outside the window, and when something lands on it I make myself put down whatever I am holding and look. I am not good at it yet. I confuse the sparrows. But last spring a bird came that stopped me cold, slate-gray, rust along the flanks, a smooth dark cap, flicking its tail and creaking like a rusty gate.

I looked it up, which he would have called cheating. An eastern phoebe. Not a junco. Not a titmouse. A phoebe, which is a bird that returns to the same nesting place year after year, and which is often the first migrant to come back in the spring.

He had been describing it exactly. I simply hadn't been listening. I stood at the window a long time and I said, out loud, to no one, "You were right." And then, because it was true and because he could not hear it and because that is the shape most of our late apologies take, "I'm paying attention now."

It stayed a moment longer. Then it lifted off the feeder and was gone, the way they always are, before you are ready, while you are still deciding what to call it.
