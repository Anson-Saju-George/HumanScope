# The Prize Is Not for the Text

Literary prizes should not be open to fiction that a machine generated on its own. This is not a fear of new tools or a defense of the typewriter against the word processor. It is a claim about what a prize is for. A literary prize is not a quality-assurance stamp on a block of text; it is a society's way of honoring a person for having made something. Remove the person and you have not modernized the award. You have quietly changed what it measures, and hoped no one would notice.

Begin with the distinction that most of these arguments blur on purpose. There is a difference between AI-assisted writing and AI-generated writing, and the difference is authorship. When Rie Kudan won Japan's Akutagawa Prize in January 2024 for *The Tokyo Tower of Sympathy*, she told reporters that roughly five percent of the book's sentences came verbatim from ChatGPT. That is assistance. A writer with a governing vision reached for a tool, took what served the book, and remained the author of the other ninety-five percent and of the whole. No sane prize policy needs to forbid that, any more than it forbids a thesaurus, an editor, or a research assistant. The eligible object is still a human work.

What should be ineligible is the other thing: a text produced end to end by a model from a prompt, then submitted as though a person had written it. Here the human contribution has collapsed to a request and a selection. Someone typed "write a literary novel about grief in a coastal town" and picked the output they liked best. That is real work of a kind — it is curation, even taste — but it is not authorship, and a prize for authorship should not pretend otherwise.

The strongest objection to my position is the honest one, so let me put it at full strength rather than knock down a weak version. Prizes, the objection runs, should reward results, not biographies. Judges read blind. If a novel moves a reader, unsettles her, stays with her for a year, why should its origin disqualify it? We do not withdraw a medal because we learn the runner had unusually good genes. Art is the thing on the page; the making is the artist's private business.

This is a serious argument and it is wrong, because it misdescribes what readers actually value in fiction. We do not encounter a novel as a free-floating verbal object. We read it as an act of attention by another mind. The pleasure of literature is not only that sentences are arranged well; it is that a particular person noticed *this* about being alive and found a way to hand it across to us. When Toni Morrison writes a mother who kills her child rather than see her returned to slavery, the weight of it comes partly from the knowledge that a human being carried that moral horror in her body and chose every word to make us carry it too. Strip out the carrying mind and you keep the arrangement but lose the address. A machine can produce the shape of a confession without anyone having confessed. The result may be competent. It is not the same act, and it is the act we are honoring.

There is also a plainer problem: a prize is a scarce, positional good. Every longlist slot, every grant that follows a win, every month of a jury's attention is finite, and it is taken from someone. Give one of those slots to a generated text and you have not added a voice to the culture; you have displaced a person who spent three years and considerable private suffering making a book, in favor of an output that cost an afternoon of prompting. The economics of writing are already brutal. Prizes are one of the few mechanisms that move money and readers toward serious authors. Turning them into a contest that a well-resourced prompt operator can enter at scale does not democratize literature. It floods the one channel writers still have.

The law has already drawn this line, and drawn it in the direction I am defending. In *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, U.S. courts upheld the Copyright Office's refusal to register a work with no human author, and the Office's guidance treats the human contribution, not the machine's output, as the thing copyright protects. You cannot own a purely machine-generated text because, legally, no one authored it. It is strange to argue that a work too authorless to be copyrighted is nonetheless authored enough to win a prize for authorship. The prize would be crowning a book that, in the eyes of the law, has no author to crown.

We can already see how this ends if we let the category blur, because the visual-art world ran the experiment first. In 2022, Jason Allen entered a Midjourney image, *Théâtre D'opéra Spatial*, in the Colorado State Fair's digital-art category and won. The fair had no rule anticipating him, and the result was not a triumph for anyone; it was a mess of recrimination that helped no artist and taught the judges they had been measuring something they never defined. Literary prizes are heading for the same collision, and the lesson is not "ban technology." It is "say what you are awarding before someone forces the question for you."

None of this requires pretending the tools do not exist or will not improve. Models will get better. Someday a generated novel may be, sentence for sentence, as good as a shortlisted one — that is precisely why the eligibility question cannot be settled by quality. If we let quality alone decide, then the better the machines get, the more of the prize we hand them, until "best novel of the year" means "best output a vendor shipped this year." At that point the award has stopped tracking anything a reader cares about. The line has to be drawn on authorship, and it has to be drawn now, while it is still a choice and not a retreat.

So the practical policy is not a panic and not a purity test. Prizes should require that entrants attest to substantial human authorship, disclose material use of generative tools, and remain fully eligible when a writer used AI as a tool inside a human-authored work. Fully machine-generated fiction, submitted as authored, should be ineligible — not because it is bad, but because the prize was never a prize for text. It was a prize for the human act of writing, offered by readers who wanted to say to another person: *you saw something, and you made me see it too.* There is no one on the other end of a generated novel to say that to. Keep the prize pointed at the people who are still there.
