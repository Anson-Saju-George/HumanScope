# The Prize Belongs to the Author

Every serious literary prize rests on a quiet assumption so obvious it is rarely stated: that behind the book stands a person who made it. When we award the Booker or the Pulitzer, we are not only ranking sentences. We are recognizing that a particular human being noticed something about being alive and found a form for it. That is why AI-generated fiction should not be eligible for literary prizes. Not because the machines write badly, and not out of fear of the future, but because the thing a prize exists to honor is precisely the thing a fully AI-generated work does not have.

Let me be exact about the claim, because the debate collapses without precision. I am not arguing that no author who has ever touched an AI tool may compete. Writers already use spellcheckers, thesauruses, grammar engines, and search. In 2024, Rie Kudan won Japan's Akutagawa Prize, the country's most prestigious fiction award, for *The Tokyo Tower of Sympathy* and then told reporters that she had used ChatGPT and that roughly five percent of the book quoted the AI's output directly. Her prize was not, in my view, illegitimate. She conceived the novel, chose what to keep, bore responsibility for every line, and answered for it in public. The tool served an author. What I am arguing against is the opposite case: fiction that is generated, where the human role shrinks to a prompt and a selection, and where the substantive compositional work belongs to a model. The question a prize must ask is not "was software used?" but "whose achievement is this?"

The strongest objection to my position is the blind-reading argument, and it deserves to be met head-on rather than waved away. If a text is genuinely moving, the objection runs, why should its origin matter? Judges routinely evaluate work without knowing the author's biography; the words either land or they don't. Isn't insisting on human origin just sentimental gatekeeping? This is a real challenge, and I concede part of it. On the narrow question of whether a passage reads well, provenance is irrelevant. A beautiful sentence is beautiful whoever assembled it.

But a literary prize is not a test of whether individual sentences read well. It is an award, and awards are relational. To award something is to hold an agent responsible for it, in the way we hold a person responsible for a kindness but not a thermostat for a warm room. Consider what a prize actually does in the world. It confers honor, and honor has to attach to someone who could, in principle, deserve or fail to deserve it. We do not give medals to avalanches for their power or to sunsets for their beauty, however superior they are to any painting. The reason is not that they lack talent. It is that there is no one there to be praised. A generative model is closer to the sunset than to the novelist. It optimizes a statistical objective across a corpus of human writing; it does not choose, in any morally loaded sense, to say this rather than that. When we praise it, the praise lands nowhere. It is a gift with no recipient.

This is not mysticism about a "human soul" in art, a claim I would find hard to defend. It is a claim about what the ritual of prize-giving is for. We can see the logic already operating in adjacent institutions that have thought carefully about authorship. The United States Copyright Office has repeatedly refused to register works lacking human authorship, denying protection to an image generated autonomously by Stephen Thaler's system in the case eventually decided as *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, and stripping copyright from the AI-generated images in the comic *Zarya of the Dawn* while leaving the human-authored text and arrangement protected. The law drew exactly the line I am drawing: assistance is fine, but the parts a machine generated on its own belong to no one and are owed nothing. A prize is even more personal than a copyright. Copyright is a property right; a prize is an act of esteem. If the law will not grant even ownership to an authorless work, it is strange to grant it the higher tribute of honor.

There is a second, more practical reason, and prize administrators feel it keenly. A literary award is a promise to readers and to the field about where attention should go. Its value is scarce by design. Generative systems can produce competent fiction at effectively unlimited volume and near-zero marginal cost; a single actor could flood a submissions pile with thousands of polished manuscripts. Japan's Hoshi Shinichi Literary Award, notably, has accepted AI-assisted entries since 2013 and reported receiving several such submissions, an early signal of what scale becomes possible. A prize that treats generated work as eligible on equal terms is not being neutral. It is quietly converting itself from a judgment about human achievement into a filtering service for machine output, and in doing so it spends the very trust that made the prize worth winning. The scarcity that gives an award meaning cannot survive contact with an infinite supply.

The most honest counterargument left is the future-facing one: perhaps our concept of authorship is simply provincial, and one day a system will possess genuine intentions, a perspective, something to say. If that day comes, my argument adapts rather than breaks. The principle was never "machines are ineligible." It was "the honor must attach to whoever did the deserving." Grant a machine real authorship, real stakes in its own claims, real responsibility, and it would by that very fact stop being a tool and become a candidate. We are nowhere near that, and pretending we are there today, on the basis of systems that cannot stand behind a single sentence they produce, does not honor a new kind of author. It dishonors the old kind by awarding their prize to no one.

So the line I would draw is generous but firm. Use the tools; declare them; take responsibility for the result, and you remain a candidate, as Kudan rightly did. But the prize itself must stay reserved for work that a person can be held answerable for, that expresses a particular someone's encounter with the world, and that could, in principle, deserve the esteem we are about to pay. Fiction that a machine generated fails that test not because it is bad but because there is no one home to receive the honor.

A prize is a handshake across the distance between a writer and a reader, an acknowledgment that someone was there and made this on purpose. Extend that hand to a generated text and it closes on nothing. Keep it human, not to shut the door on the future, but to keep faith with the one thing the whole enterprise was ever about.
