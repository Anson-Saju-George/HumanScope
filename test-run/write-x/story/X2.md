# The Last Watch

The men from the Commission had left at four, their van grinding up the gravel track with the old generator strapped in the back like a hostage. They had shaken Tomas's hand and called the new system "robust." The word stayed with him all afternoon. He had been called many things in thirty-one years on Carrow Point. Robust had never been one of them.

At dusk he climbed the hundred and twelve steps anyway.

There was no need. The new lamp would start itself at 19:42, when a sensor the size of a matchbox decided the light had gone. A computer in Aberdeen would check the result. Nobody needed Tomas in the lantern room, and he knew it. His knees reminded him on every landing. But he had climbed these stairs at dusk for eleven thousand nights, give or take, and he wasn't going to skip the last one.

The lantern room smelled wrong. For three decades it had smelled of paraffin and brass polish and wet wool. Now it smelled of new plastic and the faint ozone of the control box, which hummed on the wall like a fridge. The great Fresnel lens was still there. They had decided it was heritage. It sat in its cradle, gone dim, and the new LED array sat inside it like a coin dropped into a chandelier.

He took out the logbook. It was the ninth volume. The first eight were in a box by the door, waiting for a museum in Inverness that had written him a kind letter. He uncapped his pen.

*19:10. Wind SW, force 4. Visibility good. Sea moderate.*

He always wrote the weather first. Marta used to tease him about it, back when she still came up with tea. "You write to the sea like it's a pen pal," she'd said. After she died he'd kept writing the weather, and sometimes a bit more, the kind of thing you'd put in a letter.

At 19:42 the sensor clicked. The lamp came on with no warm-up at all. The old light used to build slowly, a glow and then a gathering, like someone clearing their throat before speaking. This one was simply on, white and exact, and the lens took it and threw it out across the water in the same long sweep as before. Every ten seconds, two flashes. Carrow Point's signature. At least they hadn't changed that.

Tomas watched the beam go out over the dark. Somewhere out there was a trawler, maybe two, and a container ship heading north to the Pentland Firth. None of them would know tonight was different. That was the point. For thirty-one years his job had been to make sure nobody noticed him, and the machine would do it better.

He sat on the stool until nearly midnight. He didn't fetch anything or wipe anything down. Once he got up to trim a wick that wasn't there, and laughed at himself, a short dry sound in the round room.

Around one, a light blinked back from the north: a small boat, a torch flashed three times toward the tower. Probably a fisherman he knew, Callum Reid or one of the Macleod boys, who'd heard about the handover at the pub. Tomas stood, took the old signal lamp from its hook, and flashed three back. His hands were not quite steady.

Then he opened the logbook one last time.

*01:04. Wind easing. Saw a friend.*

He capped the pen, set the book on the sill where the next person, if there ever was one, would find it, and started down the hundred and twelve steps. Behind and above him the light kept turning. It didn't need him to.
