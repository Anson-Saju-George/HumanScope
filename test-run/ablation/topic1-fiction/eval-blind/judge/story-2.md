The kettle took eleven minutes to boil on the paraffin ring, and Aldous Merrow had long ago stopped resenting it. Eleven minutes was time enough to fill the log, or to stand at the gallery rail and count the gannets coming in low off the water, or to do nothing at all, which was a thing he had gotten good at over forty years and would now, presumably, have to unlearn.

The men from the Board had left a folder on the table. It sat there squarely, the way official things sit, and he had not opened it. He knew what was inside. Diagrams. A schedule. A photograph of the sealed grey unit they had bolted to the lamp housing that afternoon, humming to itself with the smug confidence of something that would never need a cup of tea. In the morning a launch would come and take him off, and the light would go on shining without him, as though he had been the difficulty all along.

He carried his tea up the spiral. The steps were worn into shallow bowls at their centres, dished by boots — his, and Halloran's before him, and before Halloran a line of men going back to when the tower was built by hand on the reef, stone hauled up on ropes in weather that killed two of them. Their names were on a brass plate in the entryway. He passed it every day and had stopped reading it the way you stop hearing your own clock.

At the top the lamp was already turning.

He set his tea on the sill and watched it go round. The lens was the thing he loved, if love was the word, and he had never found a better one. A great tiered drum of glass, each ring cut and polished a century and a half ago in a workshop he would never see, assembled here piece by piece so that a single flame — and later a bulb, and now this — could be gathered and folded and thrown twenty miles out to sea in a beam you could lean your whole weight against. Ships had turned on that beam. Men had lived because of it, had gone home, had grown old and told grandchildren nothing about the night the coast showed them where it was. He had done that. Not him exactly. The light. But he had kept the light.

The beam swept the wall, swept his face, went out to the dark water, came back.

He drank his tea and did the round he had done ten thousand times, though there was nothing left in it now to matter. He checked the glass for the salt haze that crept in on the westerlies and wiped a place near the base with the cloth he kept in his back pocket, more from habit than need. He noted the wind. He looked at the sea, which was doing what it always did, which was refuse to be looked at properly — always moving, never still enough to hold.

In the log he wrote the date, the hour, the wind from the southwest at fifteen, visibility good, sea moderate. Then he held the pen a moment over the ruled line and did not write the other thing. The log was for facts. He had been strict about that his whole life, had scolded a young relief keeper once, decades gone, for putting a joke in it. A log is a record, he'd said, not a diary. The boy had flushed and never done it again, and Aldous had felt like a small man about it ever since, though he had been right.

He wrote nothing about the last of anything. He blotted the entry and closed the book.

Down in the living quarters the wireless gave him the shipping forecast at the usual hour, the voice moving around the coast the way it always did, sea area by sea area, a litany he could have recited in his sleep and sometimes did. Fair. Good, occasionally poor. He listened to the whole of it though only his own patch concerned him, because the shape of the thing was a comfort, all those far waters named and accounted for, the whole dark edge of the country spoken over once more before the night closed.

He ate bread and a tin of pilchards standing up at the counter. He washed the plate and the fork and the knife and set them in the rack. He wound the clock. These were not decisions. His hands did them.

Toward the middle of the night he went up again, because he could not sleep and saw no reason to pretend, and he sat on the little stool by the lamp with his coat over his knees and watched the beam go round and let himself think about Ellen, which he did not often do, because there was a discipline to that too.

She had come out only twice in all the years. It was no place for her, they had agreed, without ever quite saying it, and she had made a life on the mainland that fit her the way the tower fit him, and they had loved each other across the distance in a way that people now would probably call a failure and that he called, privately, the truest thing he had. She had died in the spring. He had gotten the letter three days after the funeral because of the weather. He had read it at this same rail with the beam going over him and had not wept, and had wondered since whether that was strength or just the tower having finally worn the soft parts of him down to stone, like the steps.

The machine in its grey box did not care about any of that, and there was a mercy in the thought, he decided. It would not sit up on the nights it couldn't sleep. It would not stand at the rail counting birds or naming the sea areas to itself for company. It would not remember. And so nothing would be lost in it, because there was nothing in it to lose. Whereas in him — he looked at the lens turning, turning — in him there was rather a lot, and none of it wanted, and no shelf to put it on.

He must have slept, because he woke cold with the coat slid to the floor and the grey coming up in the east, that thin unglamorous dawn light that made everything look like a photograph of itself. The lamp was still turning. It would turn for another twenty minutes and then the timer — the old timer, his timer — would put it out for the day, and this would be the last time his light went dark by his own arrangement. After tonight the box would decide.

He stood, stiffly, and folded the coat over his arm. He put his hand flat against the brass of the pedestal, which was cold and beaded faintly with the night's damp, and he left it there a moment, the way you might leave a hand on the flank of a horse you were selling.

Then the beam went out.

The sudden ordinariness of the daylit room was almost shocking — just glass and brass and a tired man in a cold tower, the sea outside going about its enormous indifferent business. He picked up his empty cup from the sill. He would want to wash it before the launch came. They would tell him to leave it, that it didn't matter now, that someone would see to the place. But he would wash it and dry it and put it in the rack, because a thing done properly is done properly to the end, and because his hands, at least, still knew what they were for.

He went down the spiral, past the brass plate with the names on it, and did not read them, and went to put the kettle on. Eleven minutes. Time enough.
