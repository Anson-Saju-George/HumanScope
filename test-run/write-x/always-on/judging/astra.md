**B1**

Readable and well organized, but it constructs an entire operational history around four supplied facts. Its apparent depth is largely unsupported.

Worst slop: **“the tool you use to track work shapes the work more than you’d expect,” “The problem wasn’t Jira. It was us using Jira,” “That sounds minor. In practice…”** and **“It’s a real tradeoff and we don’t want to gloss over it.”** These repeatedly announce significance instead of demonstrating it with verified evidence.

Unsupported additions:

- **Diagnosis and external claims:** Jira’s heaviness serves large organizations; those requirements explain this team’s difficulties; the problem was the team’s use of Jira. “Jira is a capable product” is evaluative framing, while large-organization adoption is an external factual claim.
- **Feedback history:** Both complaints repeatedly arose in retrospectives.
- **Specific delays and frequency:** Opening tickets, changing status, and locating boards were slow; engineers performed these actions “dozens of times a day.”
- **Behavior and consequences:** People worked around the friction, batched updates, let tickets become outdated, and ended up with a board that no longer reflected reality.
- **Grooming history:** Sessions kept growing; the team spent them filling fields, reconciling custom workflows, and arguing about issue types. Configuration came from an earlier version of the team and had gone unmaintained. People increasingly disliked the sessions.
- **Hypothetical estimate:** Reconfiguring Jira could have occupied a quarter. This is not a claim that a quarter was actually spent, but it is still an unsupported estimate.
- **Expanded product claims:** Almost everything in Linear is mouse-free; creating, assigning, prioritizing, and moving issues takes a few keystrokes; the application responds “instantly.” Keyboard-first was supplied; that scope and performance guarantee were not.
- **New outcomes:** People now keep issues current because updates are easier. Grooming focuses on scope, estimates, and ownership instead of metadata; refinement sessions are noticeably shorter.
- **Planning history and mechanics:** The team already worked in roughly **two-week** blocks. Automatic rollover eliminated manual sprint cleanup. Commitment, completion, and slippage are visible without manually building a view. These combine unprovided product mechanics with invented team practices.
- **Additional selection rationale:** Less configuration was a benefit; Linear’s defaults and encouragement to follow them suited the team. This third rationale was not supplied.
- **Reporting history and scope:** Reporting was the *biggest* cost. The team had built dashboards and reports over years, used them for planning and external updates, and lost particular custom views and historical reports. Some historical data failed to migrate usefully—a separate migration problem absent from the brief. Linear’s cycle and project insights are another added product claim.
- **Entire mitigation story:** The team audited report usage, dropped unexpectedly many unused reports, rebuilt others, adopted exports, began working toward a longer-term solution, and informed affected stakeholders about changes and timing.
- **Unprovided endorsement:** They would switch again; speed and a less unpleasant planning process outweigh the reporting loss. Greater board accuracy and recovered grooming time are asserted as demonstrated benefits.

The trial recommendation and conditional reporting warning are advice, not invented company events. The problem is that the surrounding first-person evidence is presented as established history.

**B2**

The strongest blog overall: clear reasons, a visible downside, and less repetition. Nevertheless, it still needs substantial factual review.

Worst slop: **“Jira can do almost anything,” “The problem wasn’t Jira’s features. It was the friction,”** and **“updating an issue now takes about as long as thinking about it.”** The last phrase substitutes a catchy exaggeration for an observed result.

Unsupported additions:

- **Diagnosis:** Jira’s features were not a complaint. The brief identifies two problems but does not exclude others. “Can do almost anything” is unsupported product puffery.
- **Specific experiences:** Opening tickets, changing status, and finding something mentioned in standup were the slow actions. Standup is an assumed team practice.
- **Behavioral consequences:** Delays discouraged accurate updates. The following tracker aphorism implies an accuracy problem, although it does not explicitly document how inaccurate this team’s tracker became.
- **Grooming details and comparison:** Sessions were a chore; tidying tickets, fixing fields, re-sorting, and clearing stale items consumed **more time than deciding what to build**. That comparative claim is stronger than “too long grooming tickets.”
- **Expanded keyboard claims and outcomes:** The listed everyday actions are mouse-free; updates are effectively instantaneous; the tracker stays current without reminders. The first is an elaboration of keyboard-first; the latter two invent performance and behavior.
- **Existing planning process:** The team already used short, fixed blocks. Linear made that rhythm the default instead of requiring configuration. Automatic rollover is an added product claim. Reviewing cycle contents replaced rebuilding sprints from scratch—an invented before-and-after workflow.
- **Results:** Planning became lighter; grooming continues but no longer consumes the meeting.
- **Reporting duration:** The team had relied on Jira reports “over the years.” No length of Jira use was supplied.
- **Unprovided judgment:** The team accepted the reporting tradeoff, would switch again, and considers increased speed and reduced process overhead worth more than the missing reports.

The warning to reporting-dependent readers is sensible conditional advice. It does not justify assuming that the owner has already reached the positive verdict supplied here.

**B3**

A visibly unfinished draft. It deserves credit for leaving unknown reporting details blank, but that restraint is inconsistent.

Worst slop: **“We didn’t do it because Linear was new or because someone saw a good demo,” “Our backlog sessions had turned into their own job,”** and **“less time on process and more time shipping.”** The opening invents rejected motivations just to create a rhetorical contrast.

Unsupported additions:

- **Motives and feedback:** Newness and a good demo were not reasons for switching; both complaints repeatedly surfaced.
- **Tolerance, actions, and frequency:** The team could tolerate occasional lag; filing bugs, moving tickets, and checking teammates’ work incurred clicks and page loads; these actions happened “dozens of times a day.”
- **Behavior:** People put off opening Jira.
- **Grooming specifics:** Tidying, re-estimating, and re-sorting consumed more time than deciding what to build. “Their own job” and inability to spare that time are rhetorical intensifications, not supplied measurements.
- **Expanded keyboard claims:** The listed actions can be performed without a mouse, and updates take a couple of keystrokes.
- **Update habits and improvement:** People now update more frequently and continuously, having previously saved updates for standup.
- **Planning mechanics and history:** Cycles are fixed time boxes with automatic rollover; the team already followed a similar rhythm and required no process adjustment. The latter is a plausible explanation of “fit,” but remains unconfirmed history.
- **Results and a specific process change:** Ticket management and grooming take less time. Reviewing the backlog during cycle planning replaced a separate long meeting. That replacement is a substantial invention.
- **Unprovided endorsement:** They would switch again; speed and cycle fit outweigh reporting losses.

The author, role, date, cycle length, report names, workaround, and closing invitation are **explicit placeholders, not fabricated facts**. Likewise, a suggested manual export is not a claim that an export was implemented. But those blanks prevent the piece from functioning as a finished company post. “For now we’re handling that gap by…” also remains an unresolved assertion until the owner supplies the actual situation.

Advice about other teams’ possible decisions is conditional framing, not invented evidence.
