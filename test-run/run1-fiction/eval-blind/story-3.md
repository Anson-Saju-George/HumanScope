At four in the afternoon, Vale carried the brass oil can up the tower, though there was nothing left to oil.

He knew this before he started. The new motor had sealed bearings; a yellow label said so in three languages. The mainland men had fitted it on Monday, along with the gray cabinet downstairs and the little glass eye on the north wall.

Still, the oil can belonged on its hook beside the lantern-room door, and it was not on its hook. He carried it up.

The afternoon was clear enough to see the farms beyond the strait. Their fields made squares of faded green between stone walls. Nearer, gulls stood along the shed roof with their heads all pointing inland. The weather glass had fallen since breakfast.

Vale hung the can and wiped the place where his thumb had marked it. Then he went around the lens with a linen cloth.

The lens did not need wiping either. It had been cleaned yesterday for the inspector. Afterward, the younger technician had leaned close and said, “Imagine making all that by hand.” His breath had clouded one prism.

He worked by sections, as he always did, following the curve. The great rings took the late sun and broke it across his sleeves. On storm evenings they caught the light from below and sent it outward, but in daylight they showed the lantern room back to him in pieces: window, sea, red railing, his own hand, window again.

At five he descended to the kitchen and put the kettle on.

Most of his things had gone over in the supply boat: two crates of books, his good coat, the wireless, the framed photograph from above the mantel. Tomorrow, when the launch came for him, he would take one valise. The rooms had begun to sound wrong. Cupboards answered too clearly when he shut them.

On the table lay the station ledger, open to the last page. He had ruled the date in blue pencil that morning.

Wind WSW, he wrote. Sea moderate. Visibility good, becoming fair.

He left the remainder blank.

The kettle clicked faintly before it boiled. He lifted it off. There were two cups on the draining board. One had a blue band around the rim, chipped where a handle joined the bowl. He put that one in the cupboard and made tea in the white cup.

At half past five, the telephone rang.

“We can see you from the office,” said Baines, the harbor master. “Or the top of you, anyway.”

“That’s reassuring,” he said.

“Forecast says rain by midnight. Launch at seven in the morning, weather allowing.”

“It’ll allow.”

Baines was quiet. The wire made its small oceanic sound.

“You’ve got the instructions for the panel?” he asked.

“The panel has instructions for me.”

Baines laughed. “Don’t switch it over tonight. Inspector wants the handover clean.”

“I know.”

“Right. Well. See you in the morning.”

Vale held the receiver for a moment after the click, then returned it to the cradle.

At six twelve, the sun touched the low cloud. He put on his jacket and climbed.

The tower steps turned ninety-three times. When Anna still climbed them, she would stop at the forty-seventh and claim there was a draft no one had accounted for. He had told her there were drafts on every step.

There was no draft at forty-seven tonight. The slit window there had been sealed with fresh putty.

In the service room, he checked the breakers. In the lantern room, he drew the curtains from the lens one by one. The air had cooled. Far below, a line of foam whitened against the reef, vanished, and came again.

At six twenty-six, by the clock above the door, he switched on the lamp.

It began without ceremony. Light entered the prisms and the panes went black around it. The lens turned, slow and soundless on the new motor, and Long Head answered from twenty-one miles south: three white flashes, pause; three white flashes, pause.

Vale stood with one hand resting on the railing. His own station sent one long beam over the strait, followed by two short. The white swept the farms, the water, the red railing, then came back through the glass and passed over him.

Once, during his first winter, he had forgotten to wind the weight until the warning bell struck. He had been twenty-seven. He had run down two flights, wound with both hands, and spent the rest of the night listening for a bell that did not ring. He had never written that in the ledger.

Now there was no weight to wind. The old chain had been lifted from the shaft and coiled in a box for disposal. Vale went down anyway and put his palm on the place where the winding handle had polished the wall.

He ate bread and cold mutton at the kitchen table. Rain arrived at ten, a dry scattering at first, then steady drops against the eastern windows. He washed his plate and set it upside down to drain. From the drawer he took the cribbage board, found three pegs still fixed in their holes, and put it back.

The log required an entry at midnight. He made the round slowly, carrying the hand lamp through the passage and out to the generator shed. The backup engine showed a full tank. The battery rack showed charge. The fog signal stood ready, although the visibility had held. At the north wall the glass eye shone dully under the hood the technicians had taped over it.

AUTOMATIC CONTROL, said the cabinet inside. Below this, in smaller letters: DO NOT OBSTRUCT SENSOR.

Vale checked the tape at each corner.

At midnight he wrote: Rain. Visibility fair. Light operating correctly.

He considered the word correctly, then blotted the line and closed the book.

Sleep was not expected. He sat in the watch room with his boots off and heard water travel through the gutters. The empty shelves had left pale bands on the wall. Above the stove, four pencil marks remained beside the doorframe. A painter from the department had once offered to cover them, and Vale had said the wall was damp there. The painter had shrugged and moved on.

The highest mark had 14/8 beside it in Anna’s hand. It meant August 14, though no one looking at it now would know.

Around three, the rain thinned. Vale dozed in the chair and woke because the tower had gone silent.

He was on his feet before he understood. Then the beam crossed the window: one long, two short. The new motor made so little sound that sleep had erased it entirely.

He climbed to the lantern room.

Water pearled on the panes. The light revolved inside its crystal house, patient and exact. A moth battered itself against the glass near the ceiling, throwing a loose shadow through the lens each time the beam came around.

Vale found the long-handled brush kept for cobwebs and held it up. The moth climbed onto the bristles. He carried it down the stairs, one careful turn at a time, past the sealed slit at forty-seven, past the service room, past the cabinet with its covered eye. At the kitchen door he shook the brush into the wet grass.

The moth disappeared. It might have flown or fallen; he could not tell.

By six, the eastern cloud had separated into gray bars. Vale put on his boots and climbed for the last entry. Beyond the glass, the farms returned field by field. The sea lost its blackness. Long Head’s three flashes faded until he could no longer be sure he saw them.

At six fourteen, he turned off the lamp.

The lens continued to revolve. Without the light inside it, it gathered the room: the paling windows, the red rail, the brass oil can on its hook. Vale watched it make one full turn, then another.

Downstairs, he wrote the time in the ledger and signed beneath it. He did not fill the rest of the line.

The launch sounded from the harbor at seven. Vale buttoned his coat, picked up the valise, and went as far as the kitchen door. Then he set the case down and crossed to the cupboard.

He took out the cup with the blue band.

Outside, Baines was tying up at the landing. Vale placed the cup upright in the valise between two folded shirts. He locked the station door and tried the handle once.

As they pulled away, the tower stood pale against the clearing sky. Halfway across the strait, Baines looked back.

“Thought they’d have it going,” he said.

“Too bright,” Vale said.

The lantern panes flashed once in the sun, then were only glass.
