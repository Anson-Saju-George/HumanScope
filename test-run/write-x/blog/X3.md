# Why We Moved From Jira to Linear

Last quarter, our 12-person engineering team moved off Jira and onto Linear. We'd talked about it for a while, and when we finally did it, the move took less effort than we'd feared. This post covers why we switched, what got better, and what we gave up.

## The problem wasn't Jira. It was how Jira made us work.

Jira is a capable tool, and plenty of teams run well on it. For a team our size, though, it had started to feel heavy.

Speed was the first problem. Opening an issue, switching boards, or changing a field meant waiting for a page to load. A few seconds doesn't sound like much, but it happened dozens of times a day, per person. People began batching their ticket updates to avoid the waits, so the board was usually a little out of date.

The bigger problem was grooming. Our backlog refinement sessions kept getting longer. We spent the time on custom fields, workflow states, component tags, and ticket descriptions written to fit a template, not on deciding what to build. Somewhere along the way, keeping the tracker tidy had become a job of its own, and it was eating time we wanted to spend shipping.

## What drew us to Linear

**It's keyboard-first.** Almost everything in Linear has a shortcut. You can create an issue, assign it, set its priority, and move it to a new state without touching the mouse. Most of our engineers already live in the terminal and their editor, and Linear feels like an extension of that setup. Updating an issue now takes about as long as saying what changed, so people do it as they go and the board stays current.

**Cycles match how we work.** We already worked in two-week chunks, and Linear's cycles fit that without much configuration. Unfinished work rolls into the next cycle automatically. That removes a surprising amount of end-of-sprint bookkeeping and gives us an honest picture of what carried over and why.

**It has fewer knobs.** Linear is opinionated about workflow, and we've found that useful. There are fewer fields to fill in and fewer ways for two people to file the same kind of work differently. Our grooming sessions are shorter now and mostly about priorities, not ticket hygiene.

## How the migration went

We imported our open issues and left most of the closed history in Jira as a read-only archive. Rather than recreate our old Jira workflow, we started from Linear's defaults and changed them only where something actually got in our way. Most people were comfortable within the first week. The shortcut cheat sheet got a lot of use early on.

## What we gave up

Reporting is the main thing we lost. Over the years we'd built a set of Jira dashboards and reports that our leads and some stakeholders outside engineering relied on. Some of them were detailed breakdowns by component, custom velocity views, and cross-project rollups. Linear's built-in insights handle the basics well, but they don't do everything our Jira setup did.

We're handling that in a few ways:

- We looked at which reports people actually opened, and some of them turned out not to matter much anymore.
- For the ones that do matter, we're pulling data through Linear's API into lightweight reports of our own.
- We're being upfront with stakeholders that some views look different now, or show up less often, while we rebuild them.

If your organization depends heavily on detailed tracker reporting, this is the tradeoff to look at hardest before you switch.

## Would we do it again?

Yes. For a team of our size and working style, getting speed back and spending less time grooming was worth the reporting gap. The tracker now takes less of our attention, and that was the point of switching.

Linear won't suit every team. Larger organizations with complex cross-team dependencies, or with strict reporting and compliance needs, may be better served by Jira's flexibility. But if your team is small, moves fast, and feels like it spends more time managing tickets than closing them, Linear is worth a trial cycle.
