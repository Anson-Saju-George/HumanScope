# Continuity diagnosis

Mode: DIAGNOSE (no rewrite). There is one real continuity break: Anna knows what the letter is before she opens it. The other points below were checked and are fine.

## 1. Anna knows the sender before she opens the envelope (main problem)

- **Evidence.** Paragraph 2: "she decided that tomorrow she would finally call the adoption agency whose letter was waiting, unopened, in the hall." Paragraph 3: "She opened the envelope after dinner. It was from an adoption agency in Leeds. She read it twice before she understood that it was about her."
- **Intended effect.** Paragraph 3 is written as a discovery. Anna, and the reader, find out who the letter is from only when she opens it, and "before she understood that it was about her" depends on her not knowing.
- **Failure.** In paragraph 2 she already knows the unopened letter is from an adoption agency. She has also apparently been meaning to call them ("finally"). That undercuts the reveal in paragraph 3 and makes her slow understanding hard to believe. The line also sits in close third person on Anna, so it can't be read as the narrator knowing more than she does.
- **Suggested fix.** Cut the adoption-agency clause from paragraph 2 and end the sentence with the sister and the funeral. If you want the letter on her mind there, point to it without naming the sender, e.g. her thoughts go back to the blue envelope in the hall. If the foreknowledge is intended, rewrite paragraph 3 as confirmation instead of discovery ("It was the agency, as she had guessed...") and drop "before she understood."

## 2. How could she identify the sender at all?

- **Evidence.** Paragraph 1 describes "a thin envelope with no return address." Paragraph 2 names the sender while the envelope is still "unopened."
- **Intended effect.** The anonymous envelope sets up the mystery and makes it believable that she "had almost thrown it away with the catalog."
- **Failure.** With no return address there's nothing on the envelope to tell her who sent it. So paragraph 2 contradicts the physical detail in paragraph 1 as well as paragraph 3. "Almost thrown it away" also only makes sense if the letter meant nothing to her yet. You wouldn't nearly bin a letter you were planning to act on.
- **Suggested fix.** The fix for problem 1 solves this too. If you keep the foreknowledge, give her a reason to know, e.g. a postmark or an earlier letter or call, and reconsider "almost thrown it away."

## Checked and consistent (no change needed)

- **Timeline.** She gets home at six, puts the kettle on, has dinner, then opens the letter. The order holds.
- **The mail's location.** The letter is on the hall table in paragraph 1 and "in the hall" in paragraph 2. That's consistent.
- **Flash-forward.** "She would remember, years later..." fits a discovery later that evening, and supports reading paragraph 3 as the real reveal.
- **Rain, sister, father's funeral.** None of these is contradicted anywhere in the scene. The rain hasn't been mentioned before, but that's new information, not a continuity error.
