# The 1919 Boston Molasses Flood

On the unusually mild afternoon of January 15, 1919, a wave of molasses roughly two to three stories high tore through the North End of Boston. It killed 21 people, injured about 150, flattened buildings, and buckled part of an elevated railway. The disaster sounds absurd—death by dessert syrup—and for decades it was treated as a local curiosity. But the flood was a genuine industrial catastrophe with a clear cause, a hard-fought legal reckoning, and a lasting influence on how the United States regulates the things engineers build. Here is what happened, why, and why it still repays attention more than a century later.

## What happened

At the corner of Commercial Street, near the waterfront in Boston's crowded, largely Italian-immigrant North End, stood a steel storage tank owned by the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol (USIA). The tank was large—about 50 feet tall and 90 feet across—and on that day it held roughly 2.3 million gallons of molasses, some 26 million pounds of it.

Shortly after 12:30 p.m., the tank failed. Witnesses reported a rumble, a sound like machine-gun fire as the steel rivets sheared loose, and then the collapse of the tank walls. A dense, dark wave surged outward. Contemporary and later estimates of its dimensions vary and should be treated as approximate, but accounts commonly describe a wall of molasses on the order of 25 feet high at its peak, spreading across a wide front and moving at something like 35 miles per hour in its first moments.

The physical damage was severe. The flood swept away or crushed nearby buildings, knocked a firehouse off its foundation, and damaged the supports of the Boston Elevated Railway. People, horses, and vehicles were caught in it. Some victims drowned or suffocated in the viscous flood; others were killed by debris or the force of the wave. Rescue was slow and grim: workers, sailors from a nearby Navy ship, police, and Red Cross personnel waded into waist-deep molasses that clung to everything. Cleanup of the district took weeks, and the harbor and streets reportedly carried a sticky residue and a sweet smell long afterward—a detail often repeated in local memory, though the more colorful claims of a lingering scent decades later are folklore rather than documented fact.

## Why it happened

The short answer is that the tank should never have held what it held, and the people responsible had cut corners from the beginning.

The tank had been erected in 1915 to feed wartime demand. Molasses was a feedstock for industrial ethanol, which in turn was used to make munitions—an important commodity during the First World War—as well as for beverage alcohol. There was commercial pressure to build fast and store as much as possible, and the timing added to it: national Prohibition was closing in (the Eighteenth Amendment was ratified that same month, January 1919), giving the company reason to move product while it still could.

The construction itself was the core failure. The project was overseen for the company by an administrator, Arthur Jell, who had no engineering or architectural training and did not have the plans meaningfully checked by qualified professionals. The steel used for the walls was too thin for the loads the full tank would impose, and the structure lacked an adequate safety margin. Later analysis pointed to steel that was, by modern standards, insufficiently tough and prone to brittle fracture, with the walls and rivet design leaving too little reserve strength. The tank was also never properly tested before being put into service. Rather than filling it with water to check for leaks and structural soundness, Jell reportedly ran only a minimal test.

The consequences were visible almost immediately. The tank leaked from the day it was commissioned. Residents in the neighborhood collected the molasses that seeped out, and the company's response was not to fix the flaw but to disguise it: the tank was painted brown, which made the ongoing leaks harder to see. For roughly three and a half years, a badly built vessel was cyclically filled and emptied, stressed and flexed, with warning signs treated as a cosmetic problem.

Weather and physics supplied the trigger and shaped the damage. In the days before the collapse, Boston had been very cold, and then temperatures rose sharply. A relatively warm delivery of molasses added not long before, sitting inside colder, older stock, may have driven fermentation and thermal expansion that raised internal pressure on an already marginal structure. Temperature also helps explain why the flood was so deadly once the wall broke. Molasses is a non-Newtonian fluid: it flows readily under force but stiffens as it cools and slows. A 2016 study by researchers associated with Harvard modeled exactly this, concluding that the initial wave moved fast enough to be inescapable, and that as the molasses cooled in the January air it grew more viscous, trapping people who might otherwise have pulled free. The substance that made the disaster sound comical is precisely what made it lethal.

## The reckoning

United States Industrial Alcohol did not accept responsibility. In the litigation that followed, the company argued that the tank had been destroyed not by its own defects but by sabotage—that anarchists had planted a bomb. The claim fit the anxieties of the era, a period of labor unrest and anarchist bombings, but it did not fit the evidence.

The many claims against the company were consolidated into a single, sprawling proceeding, one of the earliest large class-action-style cases in Massachusetts. It ran for years and involved extensive expert testimony about metallurgy, structural loads, and the tank's history of leaking. The court-appointed auditor ultimately rejected the sabotage defense and found that the disaster was the result of structural failure for which the company was responsible. USIA settled, paying out a sum generally reported as somewhere in the range of several hundred thousand dollars to the victims and their families—a large amount for the time, worth many millions in today's money, though it can never have matched the human cost.

## Why it still matters

The flood's most concrete legacy is regulatory. In its aftermath, Massachusetts and, over time, other jurisdictions tightened the rules governing how large structures are designed and approved. The pattern that mattered was requiring that engineering and architectural plans be prepared, stamped, and signed by qualified, certified professionals, and filed with public authorities for review before construction. What had been left to a company administrator with no relevant training became a matter of documented, accountable expertise. The modern expectation that a licensed engineer signs off on a major structure, and that the paperwork exists for inspectors to check, owes something to lessons paid for on Commercial Street.

There is a broader lesson in the causal chain, and it is not really about molasses. Nearly every element of this disaster is recognizable in later industrial and structural failures: commercial pressure to build quickly and store more; unqualified oversight; a testing step skipped to save time; early warning signs—here, literal leaks—managed cosmetically rather than fixed; and, when it all failed, a first instinct to blame an outside villain rather than the decisions inside the organization. Those are not quirks of 1919. They are the standard anatomy of preventable catastrophe, which is why the case still appears in engineering ethics courses and safety training.

Finally, the flood matters because of who it happened to. The dead and injured were largely working people and immigrants in a dense, poor neighborhood, living beside an industrial hazard they had not chosen and could not inspect. Their deaths forced a shift toward the idea that the public has a right to demand competence and accountability from the people who build in their midst. The story survives partly because it is strange enough to remember. But the reason it is worth remembering is ordinary and serious: a large thing was built badly, the signs were ignored, and people who trusted that it would hold were the ones who paid.
